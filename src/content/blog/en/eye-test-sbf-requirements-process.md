Many aspiring skippers trip over a simple question when signing up for the German recreational boating licence: do I actually need an eye test? The short answer is that you have to prove your medical fitness, but you have two ways to do it. This article explains what is required of your eyesight, how the medical certificate works and when a quick look in your wallet is enough because your car licence already covers it.

The **Sportbootführerschein (SBF)** is the German recreational boating licence, split into an inland version and a coastal version. Both require the same proof of fitness before you can sit the exam.

## Why you need proof of fitness at all

Anyone steering a boat has to spot other vessels, buoys, lights and signals reliably and in good time. That is why the law requires proof of physical fitness before you are admitted to the exam. This proof is one of the mandatory documents you submit along with the application form and a passport photo to the examination board. Without it, you will not be admitted to the exam in the first place.

Importantly, this requirement applies to both the [SBF Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) and the [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/). For the radio certificates alone, such as UBI or SRC, no such fitness certificate is required.

## Two ways to provide the proof

You can prove your fitness in one of two ways:

- **Medical certificate:** A doctor examines you and confirms on a standard form that you can see and hear well enough.
- **Copy of a valid car driving licence:** If you already hold a car licence, you have essentially already passed the eye test. That is why a copy of your valid licence usually counts as proof of fitness. You should have the original with you on exam day for verification.

For most applicants, the second route is the easiest and cheapest. If you have a car licence, you usually need neither a doctor's appointment nor a separate certificate. The medical certificate only comes into play if you do not hold a car licence.

## Eye test or medical certificate? The terms explained

Colloquially, many people simply say "eye test", but they mean different things by it. A look at the difference from the car licence helps to sort this out:

- For the **car licence**, you take a pure eye test, often at an optician or an officially recognised eye-test centre. This eye test only checks visual acuity.
- For the **recreational boating licence**, by contrast, the law requires a more comprehensive proof, the medical certificate. It confirms not only visual acuity but also hearing and colour discrimination, and must therefore be issued by a doctor.

So a pure optician's eye test alone is not enough for the SBF. It is only indirectly relevant because it was part of the path to the car licence, and that car licence is in turn accepted as proof of fitness. Anyone without a car licence cannot avoid the medical certificate.

## What the medical certificate checks

The medical certificate for SBF applicants follows a nationwide standard form. On it, the doctor records the results for three areas: vision, hearing and colour discrimination.

### Vision

The most important point is visual acuity. The requirement is a visual acuity of **at least 0.7 in one eye and 0.5 in the other**, in each case with or without a visual aid. That means glasses and contact lenses are expressly allowed. If you only reach these values with a visual aid, that is perfectly fine, but you should then wear that visual aid while boating too.

### Hearing

Your hearing is checked as well, though without elaborate equipment. Put simply, your hearing is considered sufficient if you can understand speech at a normal volume from a few metres away, with or without a hearing aid. In practice this is not a problem for the vast majority of applicants.

### Colour discrimination

The eye test also includes a check of colour vision, especially in the red-green range. The reason is obvious: navigation lights and many buoys work with the colours red and green. However, **red-green colour blindness does not automatically lead to exclusion**. This case is actually fairly common. The finding is simply noted on the certificate, and the doctor assesses your fitness in the overall picture.

## Who issues the certificate and what does it cost?

The medical certificate can be issued by a doctor; a general practitioner is enough. So you do not necessarily have to go to an ophthalmologist or a specialist practice. You can get the right form from the examination board or through your boating school. It is best to bring it to the appointment already filled in with your personal details.

There is no flat figure for the cost, because the examination is a self-paid service and not covered by health insurance. The amount stays within a manageable range and varies from practice to practice. It is best to ask the practice in advance what the certificate costs.

## Validity: do not get it too early

One point is easily overlooked: the medical certificate is valid for **twelve months** from the date it is issued. If you get it too early and then postpone your exam several times, the proof may expire before you even sit the exam. So schedule the doctor's appointment to match your planned registration. How the registration itself works is explained in the article [how to register for the SBF exam](/en/blog/how-to-register-sbf-exam/).

## What if I do not reach the required values?

First of all: visual aids are allowed. If you reach the required values with glasses or contact lenses, everything is fine. If you cannot reach the visual acuity even with a visual aid, or if there are other health concerns, that does not immediately mean the end. The doctor can note conditions, such as wearing a visual aid, or in individual cases recommend additional examinations. In such a case, talk openly with the doctor and with your boating school about your options.

## What to bring to the appointment

To make the doctor's visit go smoothly, it is best to bring the following:

- the **form for the medical certificate** (the standard form for SBF applicants), ideally already filled in with your personal details,
- a **photo ID**,
- your **visual aid**, if you wear glasses or contact lenses, since the values may be reached with a visual aid.

The appointment itself usually takes only a few minutes. You read an eye chart, your colour vision is briefly checked and your hearing is assessed in conversation. The doctor then enters the result and signs.

## Stricter rules apply to higher licences

The proof described here applies to the SBF Inland and Coastal. For more advanced licences that can partly be used commercially, such as the coastal skipper's licence and beyond, stricter or more extensive fitness requirements may apply. If you are planning larger passages or commercial use in the long run, find out in good time about the rules that then apply. For the classic recreational SBF, however, the straightforward proof described in this article is all you need.

## No first-aid course needed

A common misconception: many people assume that, as with the car licence, a first-aid course is mandatory for the recreational boating licence. That is not the case. No first-aid proof is required for the SBF. Basic first-aid knowledge on board is of course still sensible, but it is not a formal requirement for admission.

## How to proceed in practice

So that you do not forget anything, here is the process in short form:

1. **Check your car licence.** Do you hold a valid car driving licence? Then a copy usually suffices and you need no doctor's appointment.
2. **Otherwise book an appointment.** Without a car licence, make an appointment, for example with your GP, and get the form beforehand.
3. **Have the examination done.** The doctor checks vision, hearing and colour vision and enters the result.
4. **Attach the proof.** Submit the certificate or the licence copy with the application form and the passport photo to the examination board.

## Conclusion

The eye test for the recreational boating licence is not an obstacle but a quickly settled formality. Anyone with a valid car licence usually gets through with a simple copy. Everyone else obtains a medical certificate from their GP confirming their vision and hearing. The required visual acuity of 0.7 and 0.5 can be reached with glasses or contact lenses, and even red-green colour blindness is usually not a problem. Just make sure not to get the certificate too early, as it is valid for twelve months.
