"Should I get my Sportbootführerschein under motor, or also under sail?" This question comes up at almost every exam registration, and the answer depends less on the exam itself than on how you actually plan to be on the water afterwards. This article explains what the "Unter Segel" (under sail) endorsement really means, how it changes theory and practice, what it costs, and when the extra effort is worth it.

## SBF Inland and SBF Coastal treat sailing differently

The most important distinction up front: the **SBF Inland** and the **SBF Coastal**, Germany's two sport boat licenses, do not treat sailing the same way.

The [SBF Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) can be obtained under **motor**, under **sail**, or in both scopes together. "Under sail" is a separate official endorsement on the same license, not a separate certificate and not a second document.

The [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/), by contrast, is a pure motor license. There is no official "under sail" endorsement for the SBF Coastal, the exam and the official question catalog are structured identically for motorboats and sailboats in coastal waters. Anyone who wants to sail near the coast or offshore doesn't formally need a different license than someone who only skippers motorboats. In practice, the SBF Coastal is regularly recognized by charter companies and abroad for sailing yachts too, often together with the ICC. For more on how the license is recognized abroad, see [Is the SBF valid abroad?](/en/blog/sbf-valid-abroad/).

## What the "Unter Segel" endorsement on the SBF Inland means

If you want to add the "under sail" endorsement to the SBF Inland, you sit an additional sailing-specific part of the exam on top of the motor theory and practical test. This affects both the theory exam and the practical exam, and both parts are graded separately.

**Theory:** The official question catalog for the SBF Inland under motor covers 72 basic questions and 181 inland-specific questions (253 questions total). Anyone who also wants the sailing endorsement additionally studies 47 sailing-specific questions, covering topics like sail trim, points of sail, and sailing maneuvers. In the theory exam, questions are drawn from whichever question pool is relevant, and each pool is graded separately.

**Practice:** Unlike the SBF Coastal, the practical exam for the SBF Inland is split by propulsion type. For the motor scope you demonstrate maneuvers such as docking and undocking, turning, stopping, and the man-overboard maneuver. For the sailing endorsement, sailing-specific mandatory maneuvers are added, in particular docking and undocking under sail and a rescue maneuver (man overboard) under sail. If you take both scopes together, you have to pass both maneuver blocks.

**Minimum age:** The minimum age for acquiring the sailing endorsement on the SBF Inland is lower, at 14 years, while both the SBF Inland and the SBF Coastal under motor require a minimum age of 16. For all requirements in detail, see [Requirements for the Sportbootführerschein](/en/blog/boating-license-requirements/).

## Why the sailing questions are considered the hardest part

If you choose the sailing scope, plan for the fact that this exact topic block tends to have the highest failure rate across the whole SBF Inland question catalog. Points of sail, tacking and gybing, trim, and the correct response in typical sailing situations simply don't follow intuitively from everyday motorboating, they have to be learned deliberately, often best understood through hands-on practice rather than theory alone. For a closer look at failure rates across the different topic blocks, see [The Hardest SBF Exam Questions](/en/blog/hardest-sbf-exam-questions/).

If you take the SBF Inland exclusively under motor, you don't have to answer the sailing questions at all and save yourself that entire study effort. That's a legitimate and commonly chosen path if it's clear you'll only ever skipper motorboats. On the other hand, if you're learning the sailing questions anyway, say because you already have sailing experience or a sailing course under your belt, the extra effort usually feels smaller than the raw question count suggests.

## Combined exam: motor and sail together

If you already know at registration time that you'll want to skipper both motorboats and sailboats later, you can take both scopes in one combined exam instead of adding the sailing endorsement in a separate exam later. That saves not just a second registration and a second exam date, but usually also money compared with two separate exams, similar to combining the SBF Inland and SBF Coastal. The exact fee for the motor-and-sail combination is set by the relevant examination board, so it's worth checking the current fee schedule or asking directly before registering.

That said, the sailing endorsement can also be added **later** in a separate exam. You don't have to commit for good if you're still unsure at your first registration whether sailing will matter to you down the road.

## Do you actually need the sailing endorsement?

Whether the sailing endorsement is necessary first depends on whether a license is required at all. The well-known 15-hp rule for motorboats applies exclusively to motor propulsion; rules for sailboats vary by waterway. On some inland waters, larger sailboats require the SBF Inland under sail, while pure sailing is generally possible without an official license on many other waters. As soon as a sailboat also carries a motor with more than 15 hp, the regular license requirement for the motor kicks in regardless. For a full overview of where license-free boating ends, see [Boating Without a License: The 15-HP Rule](/en/blog/boating-without-license-15-hp/).

A well-known special case is Lake Constance: instead of the SBF, the Lake Constance Boat License (Bodensee-Schifferpatent) applies there, with its own category (Category C) for sailboats with an auxiliary motor, so the SBF Inland under sail isn't sufficient on that lake. Read more in [Lake Constance Boat License](/en/blog/lake-constance-boat-license/).

If you're unsure which license you actually need for which boat and cruising area, the [boating license finder](/en/tools/which-boating-license/) or the article [SBF Inland vs. SBF Coastal](/en/blog/sbf-inland-vs-coastal/) is a good starting point.

## Who benefits from the sailing endorsement, and who doesn't?

Every situation is individual, but a few broad tendencies can be named.

**The sailing endorsement tends to be worth it if you:**
- concretely plan to skipper sailboats, whether your own boat, a family boat, or a charter yacht,
- want to cruise waters where sailboats require a license,
- plan to do both eventually, motor and sail, and would rather put in the study effort once instead of a second time later,
- already bring sailing experience, say from club sailing, youth sailing, or charter holidays with a skipper.

**A pure motor license tends to be enough if you:**
- will only ever skipper a motorboat, say on a river or lake with no sailing ambitions,
- have no interest in sailing or are clearly deferring that to some future point,
- want to keep the theory study load as low as possible and would rather add the endorsement later if plans change.

## Hands-on preparation: theory alone isn't enough

The sailing questions in the catalog can be learned well with flashcards and the Boatpass app, the practical sailing maneuvers cannot. Docking and undocking under sail, the man-overboard rescue maneuver under sail, and confidently handling tacks and gybes all require real practice on the water. If you have no sailing experience yet, budget enough time for practical preparation, ideally with a sailing school or an experienced sailor on board, rather than encountering a sailboat for the first time shortly before the exam.

The reverse applies to theory: studying the sailing questions in isolation, rather than mixing them in with the already extensive inland questions, makes it easier to track which topics you've mastered and which are still open. ## Motor and sail in everyday boating: more than just an endorsement

The endorsement on your license is the formal side, but in everyday boating, motorboats and sailboats constantly meet, and the rules differ clearly. A sailboat generally has right of way over a motorboat as long as it's actually under sail. But as soon as the motor is switched on, the boat counts as a power-driven vessel and has to behave accordingly, including showing the correct navigation lights with a masthead light. For exactly how the pecking order between power-driven, sailing, and other vessels works, see [Right of Way and Collision Rules on the Water](/en/blog/right-of-way-collision-rules/); the matching lights are covered in [Navigation Lights Explained](/en/blog/navigation-lights-explained/).

These rules, by the way, are part of the basic questions and apply regardless of whether you hold the sailing endorsement, since they concern your behavior as a road user toward other boats, whatever propulsion your own boat happens to be using at the time. Anyone familiar with both propulsion types usually finds this topic block easier, since the other vessel's perspective is already familiar.

## Conclusion

Whether SBF under motor, under sail, or both is the right choice mainly depends on how you actually plan to boat afterwards. The SBF Coastal is a pure motor license without a separate sailing endorsement anyway; with the SBF Inland, you consciously choose the extra effort in theory and practice if you also want to skipper sailboats. If you'll only ever drive motorboats, you save yourself the extra sailing questions and maneuvers; if you're planning to sail, it's usually worth adding the endorsement right away instead of registering for a second exam later.
