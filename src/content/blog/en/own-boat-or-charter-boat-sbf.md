You've passed your Sportbootführerschein (SBF), the German boating license, and you're eager to get on the water, but then the next question comes up: buy your own boat or charter one instead? Both paths have their strengths, and the right answer depends less on budget alone than on how, where, and how often you actually plan to go boating. This article compares both options and highlights the SBF-related points worth keeping in mind when you decide.

## Owning a Boat: Freedom with Responsibility

Owning a boat means one thing above all: you're independent at any time. No booking calendar, no waiting for available slots, no unfamiliar equipment you first need to get used to. You know your boat, its quirks when docking, its engine, its navigation setup, and that familiarity noticeably increases safety on board. If you regularly boat in the same area, say Lake Constance, a chain of inland lakes, or a fixed coastal cruising ground, owning a boat pays off especially well.

Against that stands a set of ongoing obligations that chartering avoids entirely: berth fees, insurance, maintenance, winter storage, and the general concern about the boat's technical condition stay with you permanently. A boat also needs care even when you don't have time to go out. If you're only on the water a few weeks a year, you're still paying for the boat during all the time it sits unused at the dock.

## Chartering: Flexible, but Bound by Rules

A charter boat flips that relationship around. You essentially pay only for the days you actually use, and the charter company carries the responsibility for maintenance, insurance, and the berth. That makes chartering especially appealing if you want to explore different cruising areas, say the Baltic one time, the Croatian coast the next, a river in France after that, without having to transport your own boat there each time.

The trade-off is less routine: every charter boat is a little different, and you have to get familiar with the instruments, engine, and handling anew each time you take one over. Availability is also limited; especially in peak season, popular boats and cruising areas are often booked out months in advance. If you want to go boating spontaneously and on short notice, owning your own boat is usually the more reliable option.

## Which SBF Do You Need Where?

Whether you buy or charter, your cruising area determines which license you need. The [SBF Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) applies on German inland waterways such as rivers, canals, and lakes, while the [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/) applies on maritime and coastal waters. Neither license automatically covers the other, and depending on the boat type and engine power, one of the two may or may not be enough on its own. If you're planning to use both an inland area and the coast, it's worth considering a combined exam; you can read more about that in the article [SBF Inland vs. SBF Coastal](/en/blog/sbf-inland-vs-coastal/).

If you want to charter abroad, say in Croatia, the Netherlands, or Italy, country-specific rules come into play as well. Some countries require additional proof beyond the SBF, or only recognize the German license under certain conditions. The article [Is the SBF Valid Abroad?](/en/blog/sbf-valid-abroad/) gives an overview of this.

## What Charter Companies Ask for Beyond the License

The SBF alone is often not automatically enough for chartering every boat. Many charter companies additionally require a check-out or handover sail, where a staff member at the base checks whether you can handle that specific boat safely, regardless of the fact that you formally hold the right license. This isn't an official exam, but an internal precaution by the charter company, and it can vary depending on the provider and boat size. For larger or more powerful boats, expect that additional experience or proof may be requested, for example details about nautical miles already sailed or references from previous charters.

Important for anyone who hasn't taken their SBF yet: on certain German inland waterways, you can rent a boat under certain conditions without an SBF, using what's called a Charterschein (charter certificate) instead. How exactly that works and where the limits of this option lie is explained in the article [Charter Certificate: Renting a Boat Without a License](/en/blog/charter-certificate-rent-boat-without-license/).

## Comparing the Cost Factors

A precise cost comparison is hard to generalize, since boat size, cruising area, frequency of use, and personal expectations vary too much. As a general rule, though: owning a boat generates ongoing fixed costs regardless of actual use, for example berth fees, insurance, maintenance, and winter storage. A charter boat, by contrast, mainly generates variable costs directly tied to the days you actually use it, though the daily rate is often higher than the pure running costs of owning a boat.

If you boat infrequently but in varying locations, chartering usually works out cheaper than owning a boat that sits unused most of the time. If you boat very regularly in the same area, the fixed costs of owning a boat are often offset by the high level of use. Regardless of the boat itself, preparing for and taking the SBF exam comes with its own costs, and you'll find an overview on our [SBF cost page](/en/sbf-costs/).

## Insurance and Liability: Who's Responsible for What?

Liability also differs noticeably between the two models. With your own boat, you're responsible for taking out suitable boat liability insurance that covers damage to third parties. Depending on the cruising area, for instance when sailing in foreign waters, a valid proof of insurance can even be required just to enter a harbor or pass through a lock. You're also responsible yourself for the condition and safety of your boat; technical defects or neglected maintenance fall under your own responsibility.

With a charter boat, the charter company covers the boat's basic insurance, usually combined with a security deposit that gets withheld if damage occurs during the charter. This deposit can typically be covered through a separate damage waiver for the trip, so you're not liable for the full amount in case of an incident. As skipper, though, you still carry responsibility for safe conduct on board, for observing right-of-way rules, and for adjusting your handling to weather and visibility conditions. The charter company's insurance doesn't relieve you of your duty of care as the responsible boat operator.

## Seasonal Considerations

Another point that's often overlooked in this decision is the season. Owning a boat ties you down year-round; even outside the boating season, you need to take care of winter storage, upkeep, and possibly minor repairs, even during months when you're not out on the water at all. A charter boat, on the other hand, you book specifically for the period you actually want to spend on the water, without worrying about the time before or after.

That makes chartering especially attractive if you concentrate your boating into a few weeks a year, say during a summer holiday. If you'd rather boat regularly across several months of the year, for example on nice weekends in spring, summer, and early autumn, owning a boat pays off through its constant availability, without being tied to booking windows.

## Switching Cruising Areas: An Advantage of Chartering

If you want to experience different waters, chartering clearly has the edge. Instead of the effort of transporting a boat to a new cruising area, you simply book a suitable boat on site and let the local provider handle finding a berth, registration, and logistics. Especially for holiday trips abroad or the occasional switch between an inland area and the coast, this is often the more practical solution, even though it means getting to know a new boat every time.

You can, of course, also move or trailer your own boat to a different cruising area, but that involves extra effort and isn't practical for every boat type. For fixed, recurring trips in the same waters, owning a boat still tends to be the more convenient choice, since you don't need a new handover briefing every single time.

## A Few Questions to Help You Decide

Before you commit, it's worth taking an honest look at your own boating habits. These questions can help:

- **How often will you realistically go out each year?** A few, but intensive, trips tend to favor chartering; regular outings throughout the year tend to favor owning a boat.
- **Will you stay in one fixed area, or do you want to switch?** A fixed area favors owning a boat; switching areas favors chartering.
- **How important is familiarity with your boat to you?** If you want to rely on your own, well-known boat, owning one serves you better.
- **Do you want to deal with maintenance, insurance, and a berth, or would you rather not?** Chartering takes this effort off your hands; owning a boat requires it from you permanently.
- **Could a combination make sense?** Many license holders start out chartering to gain experience with different cruising areas and boat types, and only decide on a boat of their own later, once it's clear which type and area actually suit them.

That last option is often the most practical way in: charter first, gain experience, and only invest once you know what really fits you.

## Conclusion

Neither owning a boat nor chartering is fundamentally the better choice; both options suit different needs. Owning a boat pays off especially with regular use in the same cruising area and a desire for maximum familiarity and independence. Chartering scores with flexibility, changing cruising areas, and less ongoing responsibility, but requires you to adjust to an unfamiliar boat every time you take one over. Either way, the right SBF is the basic prerequisite, and the more confidently you master the exam content, the more relaxed every trip afterward will be, whether on your own boat or a chartered one.
