On tidal waters such as the North Sea and the Baltic, and on some rivers with tidal influence, choosing the right departure time can be the difference between a relaxed trip and a stressful one. Recreational skippers who factor current and tide into their planning not only save fuel and time, they also cut the risk of running aground or ending up in dangerous conditions at the wrong spot. This article walks through the basics of trip planning with current and tide in mind, for anyone holding a German boating license (Sportbootführerschein, SBF) or planning to sail tidal waters.

## Why current and tide shape your planning

Current has several causes: tidal streams that come with the rise and fall of the water, river current driven by freshwater inflow, or wind-driven currents. On tidal waters such as the North Sea, the Wadden Sea, or tidal river estuaries, the direction of the stream reverses with the rhythm of the tide and can reach speeds that noticeably affect your own speed over ground.

For trip planning, that means a course that would be easy to run in slack water can cost significantly more time and fuel against an opposing current, while the same course goes correspondingly faster with a following current. Knowing the direction and strength of the current lets you time your passage to work with it instead of fighting it the whole way.

## Tide tables and current atlases as planning tools

The key planning tools are the **tide table** for your cruising area and, where available, a **current atlas** (tidal stream atlas). The tide table gives times and heights of high and low water for a reference port, from which you can derive conditions at other locations in the area using correction values. A current atlas additionally shows how current direction and strength change over the course of a tide in a given area, often in hourly steps relative to high or low water at a reference port.

Our article on the [rule of twelfths](/en/blog/tides-rule-of-twelfths/) explains in detail how to estimate the water level between high and low water. For trip planning itself, current direction is a second important factor alongside water level, since the two are related but do not run exactly in step: **slack water** (when the current turns) does not necessarily coincide with high or low water in every area, and can be offset from it by a noticeable margin.

## Timing your passage with the current

The classic rule of thumb is: **run with the current wherever you can**. In practice, that means timing the start of your trip so you cover the first part of the route while the flooding or ebbing current runs in your direction, rather than fighting the current for the entire passage. On longer routes, such as along a coastline or through an estuary, it can even be worth splitting the trip into several legs, each timed to a favorable current window, instead of stubbornly sticking to a planned arrival time.

A simple example: if your course runs largely parallel to the main current direction, check when the current turns in your favor and plan your departure around it. Leaving an hour or two before the current turn that suits you will usually get you there faster and more comfortably than departing at your originally planned time straight into a building opposing current.

## Current at narrow passages and channels

Extra caution is needed at **narrow passages**: harbor entrances, river mouths, channels between sandbanks, or under bridges. Current can intensify considerably where the cross-section narrows, which makes maneuvering harder and changes how predictably your boat behaves. Before [docking or undocking](/en/blog/docking-undocking-harbor-maneuvers/) in a harbor with noticeable current, think through the maneuver in advance and consider how current and wind interact before you approach the narrow spot.

Current also matters when [anchoring](/en/blog/anchoring-basics-anchor-types-mistakes/): your boat will generally swing in the direction of whichever force, wind or current, is stronger, and that orientation can change significantly if the current reverses while you are anchored. If you don't account for this when choosing your anchoring spot and scope of chain, you risk the anchor breaking out as the boat swings.

## Current on tidal rivers

Tide and current are not only a coastal topic. On tidal rivers such as the Elbe, the Weser, or the Ems in Germany, tidal influence reaches well inland, far beyond the actual river mouth. If you are cruising such a river, it is worth knowing whether your stretch still lies within the tidal reach, since river current and tidal stream overlap there. On the ebb, river current and the ebbing tidal stream add together, which often produces surprisingly high speeds over ground heading downstream. On the flood, the incoming tidal stream works against the river current and can temporarily reverse it, so the water actually runs upstream for a while. For planning purposes, that means a downstream passage on a tidal river is almost always easiest on the ebb, while an upstream passage is considerably easier when the flood stream is running with you.

## A simple comparison: two possible departure times

A simplified example shows just how much your choice of departure time can matter. Suppose your speed through the water is 6 knots, and along your route you would face an opposing current of 2 knots at one departure time, or a following current of 2 knots at another:

- **Departing against the current:** speed over ground is only 6 − 2 = **4 knots**. A 20-nautical-mile leg would then take roughly five hours.
- **Departing with the current:** speed over ground is 6 + 2 = **8 knots**. The same leg would take roughly two and a half hours.

A difference of several hours over the same distance shows why it pays to check the tide table and current atlas before locking in a departure time. In practice, wind and sea state compound this effect further: wind against current makes the passage against the current even more uncomfortable, since short, steep waves build up, while the same wind direction tends to smooth the waves when running with a following current.

## Common planning mistakes

A few mistakes come up repeatedly when skippers factor in current and tide:

- **Only considering conditions at the starting point:** tide and current conditions can differ considerably along a longer route. Knowing the values for your home port is not enough if the trip passes through several distinct areas.
- **Forgetting a time buffer:** weather, sea state, or technical issues can delay a passage. If you plan your arrival for the very last favorable moment before the current turns, you have no margin left if things run late.
- **Looking at current and wave height in isolation:** when wind opposes current, short, steep waves can build up that are considerably more uncomfortable and potentially more dangerous than the wave height alone would suggest. Always factor this combination into your choice of route and departure time.
- **Looking at draft and water level in isolation:** especially in shallow waters or the Wadden Sea, you need to assess charted depth, tidal range, and your own draft together, not just one of the three figures on its own.

## Factoring in weather and other influences

Tide tables and current atlases describe the expected normal case, but the actual behavior of water level and current on a given day also depends on the weather. Sustained strong wind from a particular direction can effectively push water into an area or out of it, so high or low water arrives higher, lower, or at a different time than the table predicts. Air pressure matters too: pronounced low or high-pressure weather can shift the water level noticeably away from the tabulated value. For trip planning, that means not treating the [marine weather forecast](/en/blog/weather-beaufort-scale/) separately from your tide and current planning, but assessing both together, especially when wind strength and direction stay constant over several days and can therefore be expected to have a lasting effect on the water level.

## Practical steps for trip preparation

The following approach works well for planning a specific trip on tidal waters:

1. **Define the cruising area and route**, including possible narrow passages, harbors, and alternative stops.
2. **Look up the tide table and, if available, a current atlas** for the period of your trip.
3. **Identify a favorable current window** that fits the planned route, and set your departure time around it.
4. **Build in alternatives and buffer time** in case departure is delayed or the weather changes.
5. **Check the latest forecast shortly before you set off**, since wind and air pressure can shift the actual water level and current conditions away from the tabulated values.

Making these steps a habit noticeably increases your safety and peace of mind, especially in areas where tide and current play a bigger role than they do on calm inland lakes.

## Conclusion

On many waters, current and tide are not a minor detail, they are a central factor in safe, comfortable trip planning. With a tide table and current atlas as your foundation, a deliberate choice of departure time, and enough buffer for the unexpected, a trip becomes noticeably more relaxed than if you stick rigidly to a fixed clock time. Once you have internalized these basics, they will serve you well both on your own trips and on the related questions in the SBF coastal exam.
