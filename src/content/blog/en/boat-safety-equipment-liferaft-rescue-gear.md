Having life jackets on board is a given for most boaters. But what else belongs on a recreational boat so that you are not empty-handed in an emergency? This article gives you an overview of the most important **rescue equipment** and other **safety gear**, from lifebuoy and fire extinguisher to the **liferaft**, and shows you how to choose it to suit your boat and your waters. It is written with the German boating license, the **Sportbootführerschein (SBF)**, in mind.

## What is mandatory and what makes sense?

An important note first: for **privately used recreational boats**, Germany has no comprehensive, generally binding equipment list like the ones some other countries have. That does not mean everything is optional. Individual duties follow from existing rules, for example the [collision regulations (COLREGs)](/en/blog/colregs-collision-regulations-overview/) for lights and sound signals. Above all, as the **skipper you are responsible** for making sure that the boat and its equipment are fit for the planned trip. If an accident happens, people will ask whether you prepared carefully.

Four situations deserve a separate look:

- **Rental boats:** For boats rented out on German inland waters, the rental company has its own obligations. The equipment listed in the boat certificate must be on board and ready for use. Still check it yourself at handover, as described in the article on the [boat handover check](/en/blog/boat-handover-check-charter-or-own-boat/).
- **Boating abroad:** Many countries require a specific minimum set of equipment for recreational boats, often depending on the distance from the coast. Check with the authorities of the host country before your trip. The article [Is the SBF valid abroad?](/en/blog/sbf-valid-abroad/) is a good starting point.
- **Lake Constance (Bodensee):** The Lake Constance shipping regulations set their own minimum equipment requirements, for example for rescue devices. Check with the responsible authority before you go. More about this area in the article on the [Lake Constance boat license](/en/blog/lake-constance-boat-license/).
- **The exam:** The official SBF question catalog includes questions on rescue equipment, fire on board and distress signals. So what you learn here helps you on the water and in the exam.

The overview below is therefore not based on a mandatory list but on one question: **what do you need to handle typical emergencies?** These include someone falling overboard, fire, water ingress, engine failure and, in the worst case, abandoning the boat.

## Rescue equipment for people

### Life jackets for everyone on board

The foundation is a **suitable life jacket for every person on board**, in the right size for children. Which buoyancy class suits which purpose and what to look for when buying is explained in detail in [Choosing a life jacket: buoyancy classes explained](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/). The key point here: a life jacket only helps if it is worn, and an automatic one only if it is serviced regularly.

### Lifebuoy, horseshoe buoy and throw line

If someone falls overboard, something buoyant has to go into the water quickly. That is what **lifebuoys (life rings)**, **horseshoe buoys** and similar rescue devices are for. They should hang ready to hand at the stern or on the rail, not stowed away in a locker. Useful additions:

- **Buoyant line:** A line attached to a rescue device makes it easier to pull the person back to the boat.
- **Throw line or throw bag:** It lets you reach a person in the water who is not far away more accurately than a thrown ring.
- **Rescue light:** A light on the rescue device makes the person easier to find at dusk and at night.
- **Danbuoy:** At sea, a buoy with a pole and flag helps you keep the position of the accident in sight.

Practice matters as much as equipment. How the maneuver itself works is covered in the article on the [man overboard maneuver](/en/blog/man-overboard-maneuver/). Before every trip, agree who throws the lifebuoy and who keeps their eyes on the person in the water.

### Recovery: how does the person get back on board?

Many people underestimate how hard it is to lift an exhausted person in wet clothes out of the water. A **boarding ladder** that can be lowered from the water is therefore more than a convenience. Larger boats use dedicated **recovery systems** or a tackle (block and tackle). Try out in calm water how recovery actually works on your boat.

### Safety harness and jackstays

On sailing boats and in rough conditions, a **safety harness** with a **tether** and **jackstays** running along the deck are standard. Many life jackets have an integrated harness. The idea: if you are clipped on, ideally you never go overboard in the first place. That is a big safety gain, especially at night, when sailing alone or in bad weather.

## The liferaft: when it makes sense

A **liferaft** is an inflatable raft with a canopy that takes the whole crew if the boat has to be abandoned. It is carried packed in a rigid container or a soft valise and inflates itself from a gas cylinder when triggered.

### Do I need a liferaft?

On inland waters and on day trips within sight of the coast, a liferaft is unusual, because the shore or help is usually close. Things are different when you go **further offshore** or cross open sea areas. There it can take a long time for help to arrive, and the water temperatures in the North Sea and Baltic Sea make a longer time in the water dangerous. Whether a liferaft is worthwhile therefore depends on the **area, distance from the coast, season and crew**. Charter yachts for sea passages often carry one. If in doubt, ask.

### What to look for

- **Capacity:** The liferaft must be approved for the number of people on board.
- **Standard and intended use:** Liferafts for recreational boats are available built to the **ISO 9650** series of standards, with different versions for coastal and offshore use. Choose a version that matches your sailing area.
- **Servicing:** A liferaft must be inspected and repacked by an approved service station **as specified by the manufacturer, and at least every two years**. According to the official question catalog, this applies to all inflatable life-saving equipment, including automatic life jackets. You will usually find the date on the container. An overdue liferaft is a risk you should not rely on.
- **Stowage:** The raft must be quick to reach and easy to get into the water. Containers on deck are often fitted with a **hydrostatic release unit**, which frees the raft if the boat sinks.
- **Painter:** The painter (firing line) must be attached to the boat before you throw the raft, or it will drift away.

### Stay on the boat or get into the raft?

A widespread seamanship rule says, in essence: **only step up into the liferaft**, in other words only once the boat really cannot be saved. A floating boat is larger, more visible and offers more protection than any raft. Many incidents show that crews abandoned their boat too early, and it was later found still afloat. So plan before the trip who does what in an emergency: send the distress call, launch the raft, grab the emergency bag.

### The grab bag

In addition to the raft, a waterproof **grab bag** stowed within easy reach makes sense. Typical contents: handheld VHF radio, distress signals, flashlight, drinking water, first aid supplies, essential medication and a knife. The exact contents depend on your area and trip length.

## Raising the alarm and being found

In an emergency, what counts is that others quickly learn **that** you need help and **where** you are.

- **VHF marine radio with DSC:** A radio with Digital Selective Calling can send a distress alert including your position at the push of a button. You need the right radio certificate to operate it. Which one is explained in [UBI vs. SRC](/en/blog/ubi-vs-src-radio-license/).
- **EPIRB and PLB:** These beacons transmit a 406 MHz signal via the COSPAS-SARSAT satellite system in an emergency. An EPIRB belongs to the boat, a PLB (personal locator beacon) is worn on the body. Such devices must be registered or coded. Clarify this with the dealer or the responsible authority when you buy one.
- **AIS MOB transmitter:** Small transmitters on the life jacket that, when triggered, send a signal shown on nearby AIS devices. This makes it easier to find a person who has gone overboard.
- **Distress flares:** Hand flares, smoke signals and parachute rockets are basic equipment at sea. In Germany you need a **certificate of competence (Fachkundenachweis, FKN)** for certain pyrotechnic signals. More on that in [Pyrotechnics on board](/en/blog/fkn-pyrotechnic-distress-signals/), and the meaning of each signal is explained in [Distress signals at sea](/en/blog/distress-signals-at-sea/).
- **Sound signals:** At sea, under the collision regulations, vessels of 12 meters or more in length must carry a whistle. Smaller vessels do not need a whistle in the sense of the rules, but must have some other means of making an efficient sound signal, such as a horn. On German inland waterways, the inland navigation rules (BinSchStrO) apply. More in [Sound signals explained](/en/blog/sound-signals-sbf-explained/).
- **Radar reflector:** Small plastic or wooden boats are hard to see on other ships' radar. A radar reflector, mounted as high as possible, improves this significantly, especially in poor visibility.

## Fire on board

Fuel, gas, electrics and a confined space: a fire on a boat can spread quickly, and you cannot simply walk away. That is why you need:

- **Fire extinguishers** suitable for the fire classes that can occur on board, easy to reach and not mounted behind a likely source of fire. ABC dry powder or foam extinguishers are appropriate. Check the **inspection date** and have extinguishers inspected at least every two years by a competent person.
- **A fire blanket**, especially near the stove, to smother small fires.
- **Gas and CO detectors** if you cook or heat with gas or if exhaust from an engine, generator or heater can get into the cabin. Liquefied gas (propane, butane) is heavier than air, collects in the bilge and forms an explosive mixture with air.

Prevention is just as important: refuel with the engine off, wipe up spilled fuel, ventilate the engine compartment and have gas systems checked regularly.

## Water ingress and leaks

Water in the boat is a common hazard on small craft, for example from breaking waves, rain or a leaking seacock.

- **Bilge pump:** An electric bilge pump is convenient, but a **manual bilge pump** works even when the battery is flat.
- **Bucket:** Sounds trivial, but it is often the fastest way to get rid of a lot of water.
- **Leak plugs:** Tapered wooden or foam plugs are available for through-hull fittings. Many skippers tie them right next to the seacocks.
- **Sponge and tools** to find small leaks and fix them temporarily.

## Basic equipment to stay in control

Many emergencies start harmlessly: the engine fails and the boat drifts toward a shore, a weir or into a shipping channel. Then what counts is whether you can hold or move your boat.

- **Anchor with enough rope or chain**, matched to boat size and water depth. How to anchor properly is explained in [Anchoring basics](/en/blog/anchoring-basics-anchor-types-mistakes/).
- **Paddles or oars** for small boats.
- **Boat hook, mooring lines and fenders**.
- **Tow line** in case you need to be towed.
- **Tools and spare parts**, such as fuses, an impeller, a shear pin for the outboard or a spare kill cord.
- **Navigation aids**: up-to-date charts, compass and binoculars, even if there is a chartplotter on board.
- **Flashlight** with spare batteries and a **first aid kit**. What to do in an emergency is covered in [First aid on board](/en/blog/first-aid-on-board/).

## Equipment by area: a rough guide

How extensive your equipment should be depends heavily on where you go. As a rough guide, not a legal requirement:

- **Small inland lake, day trip:** life jackets for all, lifebuoy with line, paddle, anchor, bucket, fire extinguisher, first aid kit, sound signal and a charged phone in a waterproof case.
- **Federal waterways and large rivers:** additionally, anchor gear sufficient for current, a bilge pump, mooring lines and fenders for locks, and a lamp for poor visibility.
- **Coastal trips at sea:** additionally, VHF radio with DSC, distress signals, radar reflector, safety harnesses, charts and compass.
- **Further offshore or multi-day trips:** additionally, a liferaft, grab bag, EPIRB or PLB, AIS MOB transmitter and a backup power supply for radio and navigation.

## Care and checks: equipment that works

The best equipment is useless if it fails when you need it. Take time for a check at the start of the season, as described in [Getting your boat ready in spring](/en/blog/getting-your-boat-ready-spring-checklist-season-start/):

- Check **inspection and expiry dates** of life jackets, liferaft, fire extinguishers, distress signals and beacon batteries.
- Check **automatic life jackets** for gas cylinder and trigger mechanism.
- Test **bilge pumps**, including the manual one.
- Check the **radio and emergency contacts**.
- Know the **stowage places**: everyone on board should know where the rescue gear, fire extinguisher and first aid kit are.

A short **safety briefing** before casting off is part of this, especially with guests on board for the first time. Show where the equipment is, how to put on the life jackets and what to do if someone falls overboard.

## Conclusion

Safety equipment is not a checklist to tick off but an answer to the question of which emergencies can happen on your boat and in your waters. Life jackets, a lifebuoy within reach, a fire extinguisher, a way to remove water and an anchor form the basis almost everywhere. The further you get from the shore and from help, the more you add radio, distress signals, a radar reflector and finally a liferaft. Maintenance, practice and a short crew briefing matter just as much as buying the gear.
