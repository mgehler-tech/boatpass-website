A day on the water with the whole family, maybe with the dog along too, is one of the best parts of boating. At the same time, children and animals noticeably change things on board: they move less predictably, judge danger differently and are far more vulnerable in the water than adult crew. This article explains how to choose the right life jackets, which rules on board work well and how to prepare for typical emergencies involving a child or a dog.

## Your responsibility as skipper

Whoever is in charge of a boat is responsible for the safety of **everyone on board**. That applies whether you are driving a licence-free boat or a boat that requires the German recreational boating licence (Sportbootführerschein, SBF) for [inland waters](/en/sbf-inland/) or [coastal waters](/en/sbf-coastal/). With children, the parents' duty of supervision comes on top. If the parents are not the skipper themselves, agree clearly before casting off who is keeping an eye on which child.

There are no specific rules for dogs in the navigation regulations. The general duty of care still applies: an animal running loose on deck can get in your way while docking, put other people at risk or go overboard itself. So plan for the dog just as you would for another crew member who does not yet know what to do.

A good starting point is an honest question before every trip: **Can I still cope if a manoeuvre is coming up, a child is crying and the dog is pushing towards the rail, all at the same time?** If the answer is "probably not", bring another adult or choose a quieter area.

## The right life jacket for children

The German Inland Waterways Traffic Regulations (BinSchStrO) have no specific requirement for children on recreational boats; their provisions on children's life jackets apply to passenger vessels. That does not change good practice: **children always wear a life jacket on deck and in an open boat**, in the harbour too, in calm weather too, and even if they swim well.

What to look for when choosing one:

- **Weight range:** Children's life jackets are sized by body weight. An adult jacket that is too big slides up over the head in the water and does not hold the child securely.
- **CE marking and standard:** Look for a jacket to **EN ISO 12402**. Which buoyancy class suits which waters is explained in detail in our article on [life jacket buoyancy classes](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/).
- **Crotch strap:** Many children's life jackets have a strap between the legs. It stops the jacket from riding up in the water. Always fasten it.
- **Collar and head support:** Especially for small children, a collar that keeps the head above water is helpful. For babies there are specially standardised models that turn the head into the right position automatically.
- **Foam or inflatable:** Foam jackets are often the simpler choice for children, because they need no inflation mechanism and cope well with playing, climbing and falling over.

### Try it on and practise

Have your child try on the jacket at home or in the harbour before you set off. Pull upwards at the shoulders: if the jacket slides up over the chin or ears, it is too big or not tight enough. Ideally, do a **trial swim** in a pool or at a shallow swimming spot. That way your child experiences how the jacket feels in the water and is less likely to panic in a real emergency.

## Life jacket and gear for your dog

Even dogs that swim well can quickly reach their limits in the water, for example in waves, cold water or current, or when they cannot climb the smooth hull. A **dog life jacket** therefore makes sense for most boat dogs.

Worth knowing: the **EN ISO 12402 standard covers people**. With dog jackets you need to check quality and fit particularly carefully yourself:

- **Grab handle on the back:** The most important feature. A sturdy, well-stitched handle lets you lift the dog out of the water or hold it on deck.
- **Fit:** The jacket should be snug without restricting the front legs. The dog must not be able to wriggle out.
- **Buoyancy at the front:** A float under the neck and chest helps keep the head above water.
- **Bright colour and reflectors:** A dog in the water is hard to spot, especially in waves or at dusk.

Get your dog used to the jacket on land before it has to wear it on board. Many dogs accept it quickly if it is linked with something positive.

### More gear for the boat dog

- **Bowl and fresh water:** Seawater is not suitable drinking water for dogs. Bring plenty of fresh water.
- **Non-slip mat:** Paws find little grip on smooth decks. A mat in a fixed resting spot makes the dog feel secure.
- **Shade:** Below deck or under an awning, so the dog can retreat.
- **Boarding aid:** Dogs often cannot use a swim ladder. If needed, there are special ramps that let a dog get on board from the water or the jetty.

## Rules on board that children understand

Children follow rules better when they understand them and when there are only a few clear ones. Explain them before casting off, ideally on board, pointing at the places they refer to:

- **"Life jacket on before you go on deck."** No exceptions.
- **"One hand for the boat."** Whenever you move around on deck, hold on with one hand.
- **"When we dock or cast off, all children sit in their places."** Manoeuvres are the moments when you need your attention most.
- **"Hands off lines, winches and cleats while we are moving."** Lines under load can trap fingers.
- **"No sitting on the bow or the swim platform while we are under way."**
- **"If you see anything fall into the water, shout loudly and point at it."**

The last rule in particular gives children an important job. Children love taking on responsibility. You can also make them the **lookout**, have them count buoys or explain what the colours of the navigation marks mean. A child who is busy is less likely to climb where they should not be.

### Create safe zones

On many boats it is worth defining fixed areas: the cockpit is the safe place, the foredeck is off limits while under way. On sailing boats, **guardrail netting** between the deck and the lifelines can stop small children or dogs slipping underneath. For toddlers, a short **tether** with a harness clipped to a strong point in the cockpit can also make sense.

## Engine, propeller and swimming breaks

One of the biggest dangers when swimming from a motorboat is the **propeller**. A turning propeller can cause serious injuries, even at low revs. So:

- **Engine off before anyone gets into the water** or climbs back on board. Neutral alone is not enough.
- Children and dogs stay away from the stern while the engine is running.
- Use the **kill switch with lanyard** if your boat has one. If the driver goes overboard or falls, the engine stops.
- Before starting the engine, do a headcount: are all people and the dog on board?

During swimming breaks, one adult should always stay on board and watch the swimmers. Children swim with a life jacket or a buoyancy aid to EN ISO 12402-5 (50N), not with armbands, and the swim ladder must be reachable from the water. Remember that current and wind can make a boat drift faster than a child can swim.

## Sun, heat, cold and seasickness

On the water the weather hits harder than on land. The sun reflects off the surface, and the breeze from moving keeps you cool, so heat and sunburn are often underestimated.

**For children:**
- High-factor sunscreen, a sun hat with neck protection, sunglasses and light, long-sleeved clothing.
- Make sure they drink regularly, even if they do not say they are thirsty.
- **Children get cold faster** than adults. After swimming, dry them off and dress them warmly straight away. A change of clothes and a warm jacket always belong in the bag, even in summer.

**For the dog:**
- Dark decks and metal fittings can get very hot in the sun. Check with your hand whether the surface is bearable for paws.
- Dogs cool down mainly by panting, they can barely sweat. Watch for signs of overheating such as heavy panting, restlessness or staggering, and offer shade and water.
- Dogs can get seasick too. Typical signs are drooling, restlessness or vomiting.

For children, a spot in the middle of the boat, looking at the horizon and fresh air help against **seasickness**. There are medicines for children and dogs as well, but check with your paediatrician or vet beforehand and do not give anything on a hunch.

## Emergency: when a child or dog goes overboard

The emergency is the reason life jackets, rules and preparation matter so much. The basics are the same as in the classic [man overboard manoeuvre](/en/blog/man-overboard-maneuver/):

1. **Shout loudly** and alert the crew.
2. **Keep them in sight and point:** one person does nothing but point at the child or dog in the water.
3. **Throw a flotation device**, for example a lifebuoy.
4. **Keep the propeller clear:** approach so that the propeller does not come near the person or animal, and take the way off the boat in good time.
5. **Manoeuvre back calmly**, approach against wind and current and only pick them up once the boat has stopped and the engine is in neutral. Once the child or dog is at the stern, switch the engine off completely.

### Dogs: do not jump in after them

When the dog goes overboard, the urge to jump in after it is strong. That is exactly what you should not do. A person in the water is harder to rescue than a dog in a life jacket, and one emergency quickly becomes two. Instead, motor back, grab the dog by the handle of its jacket and lift it on board. With heavy dogs, two people or a ramp help.

### Children: cold, exhausted, frightened

A child who has gone overboard is usually shocked and cools down quickly. After the rescue, warmth is what counts: wet clothes off, dry off, wrap in blankets and reassure. If the child shows reduced consciousness or breathing problems, or has inhaled water, they need to see a doctor, even if they seem to recover quickly. The most important measures until then are covered in our article on [first aid on board](/en/blog/first-aid-on-board/).

## Travelling abroad with your dog and chartering

If you want to charter on holiday with your dog, clarify two things early:

- **Charter terms:** Each charter company decides for itself whether pets are allowed. Ask before booking and get the permission confirmed in writing.
- **Entry requirements:** For travel with a dog within the EU, you usually need an **EU pet passport**, a microchip and a valid rabies vaccination. Some countries have additional requirements. Check with your vet or the authorities of the destination country before you travel.

## Checklist before casting off

- Every child is wearing a **properly fitted life jacket** with the crotch strap fastened.
- The dog is wearing its **life jacket with grab handle**.
- The **rules on board** have been explained, and every adult knows who they are watching.
- Sun protection, fresh water, spare clothes and warm clothing are on board.
- A lifebuoy or throwing line is ready to hand.
- The **kill switch** lanyard is attached, if fitted.
- A first aid kit is on board, and you know where it is.
- The area suits the crew: a quiet lake rather than a busy fairway.

## Conclusion

Children and dogs on board are no reason to give up boating, but they do call for more preparation. The key elements are **well-fitting life jackets**, a few clear rules on board, an engine that is off while people swim, and a plan for the case that someone does end up in the water. If you also keep an eye on sun, cold and enough drinking water, the day on the water will be a good memory for everyone.
