You passed the exam, the license is in your pocket, and now you finally want to get going. This is exactly where typical beginner mistakes happen: the theory is solid, but the routine is still missing. This article shows where newcomers stumble most often on their first trips and how simple habits help you stay relaxed. (The **SBF**, short for Sportbootführerschein, is the German recreational boating license.)

## The license is the start, not the finish

The **Sportbootführerschein** ([Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) or [Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/)) shows that you know the rules, basic seamanship and the fundamentals of navigation. It does not replace practice. Wind, current, an unfamiliar boat or a tight harbor feel very different from an exam question.

The good news: most early mistakes are avoidable, because they almost always have the same causes. Trying to do too much, planning too tightly or ignoring warning signs puts you under pressure. Starting small and taking your time builds experience without stress.

## Mistake 1: A first trip that is too ambitious

The classic beginner mistake is a trip planned too big: long distance, unknown waters, a full boat, a tight schedule. When something unexpected happens, there is no buffer.

Better:

- Start in an **easy-to-read, familiar area**.
- Pick a **short route** with the way back in daylight.
- Plan **reserves** for time and fuel.
- Increase distance and difficulty only once you feel confident.

Nobody awards a prize for the longest first trip. A relaxed day with two clean harbor maneuvers teaches you more than a rushed long-distance cruise.

## Mistake 2: Underestimating weather and conditions

Many beginners only glance at the weather at the departure point. On the water, **wind direction and strength**, gusts, visibility and, in areas with tides or currents, the water itself all matter. Wind that seems harmless at the pier can feel much stronger on open water.

Get into the habit of:

- reading the **current forecast** and warnings before every trip (see [Using marine weather reports and warnings](/en/blog/marine-weather-report-warnings-weather-services-for-skippers/)),
- setting a **personal limit** at which you do not leave harbor or turn back,
- **postponing by a day** when in doubt rather than testing the conditions.

Turning back is not defeat. It is good seamanship.

## Mistake 3: Harbor maneuvers without a plan

Most minor damage does not happen on open water but while docking and undocking. Typical causes are too much speed, maneuvers started too late and lines that are not ready.

Helpful habits:

- **Talk it through first:** Which side will you dock on? Who handles which line? Where do the fenders go?
- **Prepare fenders and lines** before you enter the harbor, not inside it.
- **Go slowly.** A boat is much easier to control at low speed, and you can always add throttle.
- **Watch wind and current** before approaching and adapt the maneuver.

If a maneuver does not feel right, abort it, take another lap and try again. That is perfectly normal. You will find more in [Docking and undocking: harbor maneuvers for beginners](/en/blog/docking-undocking-harbor-maneuvers/).

## Mistake 4: Not knowing the boat well enough

Every boat reacts differently to rudder, throttle and wind. Especially after exam practice on another boat, the first contact with your own or a charter boat takes some getting used to.

Take time for:

- a **thorough briefing** if the boat is not yours,
- the **handover check** of papers, equipment and technology (see [Boat handover check](/en/blog/boat-handover-check-charter-or-own-boat/)),
- **practice maneuvers** in open water: driving slowly, stopping, reversing, turning in tight circles.

That way you learn how the boat responds before it matters in the harbor.

## Mistake 5: Safety equipment that just sits there

Having life jackets on board is good. Wearing them and knowing how they work is better. If the jackets lie in a locker, they do not help in an emergency.

Make sure that:

- there is a **suitable life jacket** for every person on board (see [Life jackets and buoyancy classes](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/)),
- everyone aboard knows **where the equipment is** and how it works,
- the crew gets a short **briefing**, for example on the emergency plan and on what to do when someone goes overboard (see [Man overboard maneuver](/en/blog/man-overboard-maneuver/)).

A short safety briefing before casting off takes two minutes and is part of good practice.

## Mistake 6: Rules in your head, but not in the moment

In the exam you know who has to give way. On the water the situation arrives quickly, and you need to recognize it, assess it and act. Many beginners hesitate or rely on the other vessel to give way.

What helps:

- **Act early and clearly**, so your maneuver is visible to others,
- **keep watching the situation**, not only ahead but also to the sides and behind,
- when unsure, **reduce speed** and create distance.

To feel more secure out there, refresh the basics regularly, for example with the article on [Right of way and collision rules](/en/blog/right-of-way-collision-rules/).

## Mistake 7: No plan B

What do you do if the engine will not start, the wind shifts or someone on board gets seasick? If you have not thought about this beforehand, you decide at the worst possible moment.

A simple plan B includes:

- **alternative harbors or sheltered spots** along the route,
- a working **phone or radio** and knowing how to call for help,
- someone ashore who knows your **route and return time**,
- enough **fuel and drinking water**.

Such preparation seems exaggerated until you need it once.

## Mistake 8: Forgetting the crew

As skipper you carry the responsibility. That also means looking after your passengers: Is everyone dressed properly, do they have something to drink, do they know where to sit or stand safely? Stress while maneuvering quickly spreads to the mood on board.

Good habits:

- **Announce calmly and clearly** what happens next,
- **assign tasks beforehand** instead of shouting in the harbor,
- **take breaks in time** when someone is tired, cold or feeling unwell.

This matters even more when children or guests without experience are aboard.

## Habits that make you better

Experience comes from repetition and reflection. These routines help you learn faster:

- **Review briefly after each trip:** What went well, what do you want to do differently next time?
- **Keep a simple logbook** with date, conditions and notable events.
- **Practice deliberately**, for example repeating one maneuver several times on a calm day.
- **Learn from others:** Sail along with experienced skippers now and then or attend a safety or maneuvering course.
- **Refresh your knowledge:** Rules, lights and signals fade faster than you think.

## Conclusion

The first trips after your boating license are when knowledge turns into skill. The most common beginner mistakes are overly ambitious plans, underestimated weather, unprepared harbor maneuvers, an unfamiliar boat, unused safety equipment, hesitant action, no plan B and a forgotten crew. All of them can be reduced noticeably with planning, calm and small routines. Start small, stay willing to learn and give yourself time.
