A fine after a routine check on the water is annoying but manageable. Some violations, though, turn into something bigger: losing your [recreational boating license](/en/sbf-coastal/), a criminal case, or trouble with your boat insurer. Knowing which mistakes get expensive, and why, lets you avoid them deliberately. This article gives an overview of the most common violations on German waters, how they're legally classified, and their real consequences, no scaremongering, just the facts you need.

## Regulatory Offense or Crime? The Distinction Matters

Not every violation on the water carries the same weight. Most infractions, such as ignoring a right-of-way rule or missing registration, are **regulatory offenses** (Ordnungswidrigkeiten). They're punished with a fine set by the relevant water police or waterways authority. The general framework comes from the **Act on Regulatory Offenses (OWiG)**: under Section 17(1) OWiG, a fine generally ranges between 5 and 1,000 euros, unless the specific law governing the offense sets a different amount.

For shipping, the specific law does set a different amount. The **Inland Waterways Transport Administration Act (Binnenschifffahrtsaufgabengesetz, BinSchAufgG)** allows fines of up to **10,000 euros** for violations of the Inland Waterways Order (BinSchStrO) and comparable regulations. In other words, anyone who assumes the water only carries small tickets is underestimating the situation.

Some violations go beyond a mere regulatory offense and become a **criminal matter**, for example when someone endangers others, causes bodily harm, or damages the environment. In that case, a fine or prison sentence under the Criminal Code (StGB) applies, independent of any additional regulatory fine.

## Boating Without the Required License

Operating a recreational boat when you'd need a license for it, but don't hold one, violates the relevant shipping regulations. That applies equally on inland waters and at sea, once the thresholds for engine power or sail area that require an [SBF Binnen](/en/sbf-inland/) (inland license) or SBF See (coastal license) are exceeded. The violation is a regulatory offense within the framework described above, and it can also have consequences for your insurance cover, more on that further down.

Just as frustrating: if you hold the license but don't carry it with you, that too can be flagged during a check, even though this case is usually treated far more leniently than not holding a license at all. Always bring the physical license card on board, that includes chartering: charter companies usually ask for proof before handover anyway, and if they don't, a check on the water will.

Whether your plans call for a license and your own boat, or a chartered boat with a skipper is the better fit, depends on your experience, cruising area, and budget. Our article on [owning your own boat versus chartering](/en/blog/own-boat-or-charter-boat-sbf/) weighs the pros and cons of both routes.

## Alcohol and Drugs at the Helm

One of the costliest mistakes of all is operating a boat under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Unlike on the road, there's no simple blood-alcohol fine threshold for recreational boats, the legal situation is more complex and reaches all the way to criminal liability under Section 315a StGB. We covered the topic in full detail, thresholds, and consequences in a dedicated article on the [blood alcohol limit on a boat](/en/blog/blood-alcohol-limit-boat/). The short version: anyone who operates a boat while unfit to do so and endangers others risks a fine, a prison sentence, and losing their license all at once, often made worse by losing insurance cover on top of it.

## Missing or Incorrect Boat Registration

Motorized recreational boats under 20 meters in length must carry an official or officially recognized registration number on federal inland waterways, under the Inland Waterways Registration Ordinance. Muscle-powered craft such as rowing boats, canoes, kayaks, and simple tenders are exempt. Operating without a valid registration number, failing to display it clearly, or running a boat whose registration no longer matches its current owner all risk a fine. The reason for the registration requirement is straightforward: the water police need to be able to trace a boat back to an owner if necessary, for example after an accident or another offense.

## Missing Safety Equipment on Board

Required safety equipment isn't optional either, and its absence is treated as a regulatory offense. Depending on where you're cruising, this includes life-saving equipment, sound signaling devices, fire extinguishers, and at sea, distress signal equipment as well. Our article on [life jacket buoyancy classes](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/) explains which jacket suits which situation, and our article on [distress signal equipment](/en/blog/fkn-pyrotechnic-distress-signals/) covers pyrotechnics and the certificate you need to carry them. If the equipment is missing not just on paper but in an actual emergency, the fine is the smaller problem compared to your own safety.

## Ignoring Right-of-Way and Collision-Avoidance Rules

Ignoring a right-of-way rule, for example failing to give way to a sailing vessel or maneuvering incorrectly in a narrow channel, is a regulatory offense regardless of whether a collision actually happens. If a collision does occur and causes damage or injury, what started as a simple offense quickly turns into a civil dispute over damages, and depending on severity, a criminal case as well. Our article on [right-of-way and collision-avoidance rules](/en/blog/right-of-way-collision-rules/) summarizes the key rules, they're a core topic in every recreational boating exam.

## Ignoring Speed Limits and Protected Areas

Many inland lakes, river sections, and coastal waters have their own speed limits, often combined with protected zones for nature reserves, spawning grounds, or swimming areas. Exceeding these limits or entering restricted zones risks a fine under the relevant shipping regulations or the applicable state nature conservation law. Enforcement is particularly consistent in designated protected areas, such as the Wadden Sea national parks, since both safety and conservation are at stake there. Check the specifics of your cruising area before every trip, our overview of [boating regions in Germany](/en/blog/boating-regions-in-germany/) has more.

## Polluting a Waterway: When a Fine Turns Into a Crime

One area where something seemingly minor can quickly turn serious is environmental protection. Unlawfully discharging oil, bilge water, or other pollutants into a body of water isn't just a regulatory matter, it can also be a criminal offense under **Section 324 StGB (water pollution)**. The sentencing range goes up to **five years in prison** or a fine. That applies not just to large freighters but in principle to anyone who unlawfully pollutes a body of water, including with a small recreational boat. Maintaining your engine and bilge regularly, following the guidance in our article on [basic outboard motor maintenance](/en/blog/outboard-motor-basic-maintenance/), noticeably reduces this risk.

## Duties After an Accident

A common misconception: unlike on the road, Section 142 StGB, Germany's classic hit-and-run provision, does **not directly apply** to accidents on the water according to court rulings. That doesn't mean leaving the scene after a collision has no consequences, though. The shipping regulations explicitly require everyone involved in a shipping accident to establish the consequences of the accident and disclose their identity, for example under Section 1.16(3) of the Inland Waterways Order. Violating that duty is a regulatory offense. If people are injured, criminal offenses such as failure to render assistance or negligent bodily harm can also apply. The short version: stop, exchange names and contact details, and when in doubt, inform the water police, that's always the safer choice than carrying on.

## Received a Fine Notice: What to Do Now

If a fine notice actually arrives after a check, it's worth taking a calm look at the details before you pay or object. First check whether the facts described are actually correct: which date, which waterway, which specific accusation is stated? Under Section 67(1) OWiG, you can file an objection against a fine notice within **two weeks** of receiving it, after that the notice becomes final. For simple matters, such as a registration deadline missed by a narrow margin, a straightforward inquiry to the relevant authority is often enough. If the matter involves a possible criminal offense, though, for example in connection with alcohol, an accident with injuries, or water pollution, it's worth getting legal advice early, since criminal and license-related consequences are at stake alongside the fine. Either way, don't let the deadline pass without at least checking whether an objection makes sense.

## The Consequences at a Glance

Making one of these mistakes often risks several consequences at once:

- **Fine:** depending on the violation, anywhere from a smaller amount up to the statutory ceiling of 10,000 euros under the BinSchAufgG.
- **Criminal proceedings:** possible in addition to a fine when others are endangered, bodily harm occurs, or the environment is damaged, with a fine or prison sentence.
- **License revocation:** the Generaldirektion Wasserstraßen und Schifffahrt (GDWS) decides on revocation for both the inland and coastal license alike, for example after repeated or serious violations.
- **Insurance cover:** many boat insurance policies reduce or refuse payouts for grossly negligent or intentional misconduct, such as operating without a valid license or under the influence of alcohol. Our article on [boat insurance for recreational boats](/en/blog/boat-insurance-for-sportboats-what-makes-sense/) explains what a sensible policy should cover.

Taken together, these consequences show how something that looks minor at first can quickly turn into an expensive, drawn-out problem.

In the theory portion of both the SBF Binnen and SBF See exams, a skipper's basic legal duties are regularly tested, including right-of-way rules, equipment requirements, and conduct after an accident. The [official ELWIS question catalog](/en/sbf-coastal-exam-questions/) tests less about specific fine amounts and more about understanding which duties exist and why. ## Conclusion

The costliest mistakes on the water are rarely the most obvious ones. Boating without a license, alcohol at the helm, missing registration or safety equipment, ignored right-of-way rules, and environmental violations often feel minor in the moment, but they can lead to fines reaching five figures, the loss of your license, and in the worst case, criminal proceedings. Knowing and taking these basic legal duties seriously doesn't just make you a safer skipper, it also makes for a far more relaxed one, because you know exactly where you stand.
