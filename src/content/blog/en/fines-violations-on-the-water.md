A forgotten life ring, too much speed in the wrong zone, a boating accident where you simply carry on: mistakes on the water happen fast, and they can get expensive. Unlike road traffic, German boating has no single, nationwide fine catalog. Instead, several bodies of law interact, from the inland shipping regulations to the license ordinance and the criminal code. If you hold a [Sportbootführerschein](/en/sbf-inland/) (the German recreational boating license), it pays to know which violations end as a mere administrative fine and which ones can turn into a genuine criminal offense. This article gives an overview of the most common, and most expensive, mistakes on the water.

## Why There Is No Single Fine Catalog

On German **federal inland waterways** (rivers, canals, many inland lakes), the **Binnenschifffahrtsstraßen-Ordnung (BinSchStrO)** applies. On **coastal shipping lanes** (such as the Elbe, Weser, Ems, or the Kiel Fjord), the **Seeschifffahrtsstraßen-Ordnung (SeeSchStrO)** applies instead. Recreational boats are additionally subject to the **Sportbootführerscheinverordnung (SpFV)**, which governs, among other things, when a license is required. On international waters, the **International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs)** apply.

Each of these frameworks has its own provisions for administrative offenses. The **Generaldirektion Wasserstraßen und Schifffahrt (GDWS)** is responsible for prosecuting offenses under the SpFV, while on-water enforcement is mainly carried out by the **Wasserschutzpolizei** (water police). Because so many different authorities and regulations are involved, there is no single table of fixed amounts for every violation, unlike the fine catalog you might know from road traffic. The actual fine depends on the specific violation, its severity in the individual case, and the responsible authority.

## Administrative Offense or Criminal Offense – the Key Distinction

To gauge how serious a violation is, it helps to separate two categories:

**Administrative offenses (Ordnungswidrigkeiten)** are handled under the Gesetz über Ordnungswidrigkeiten (OWiG) with either a **formal warning fine** (Verwarngeld, for minor cases handled informally) or a **fine** (Bußgeld, following a formal proceeding). A fine notice is not a criminal conviction and does not appear on a certificate of good conduct.

**Criminal offenses (Straftaten)**, on the other hand, are prosecuted under the Strafgesetzbuch (StGB) and can result in a **fine or imprisonment**. A conviction can be entered in the Bundeszentralregister (federal criminal record), making the consequences considerably more far-reaching than an administrative fine.

Many of the examples below start out as an administrative offense but can escalate to a criminal offense depending on severity, repetition, or resulting damage.

## Operating Without a Valid License

Anyone who operates a recreational boat without holding the required license commits an administrative offense under **§ 18 SpFV**, read together with the licensing requirement in **§ 3 SpFV** (inland waterways) or **§ 4 SpFV** (coastal shipping lanes), regardless of whether this happens intentionally or merely negligently, for example because someone misjudges which license their boat requires. This covers not only beginners with no license at all, but also cases where the license held does not match the waters being navigated, such as using an [SBF Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) license on coastal shipping lanes where the [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/) license would actually be required. Even skippers who hold a valid license but fail to carry it on board can run into trouble during a water police inspection.

## Alcohol and Fitness to Operate a Vessel

Alcohol at the helm is one of the most expensive mistakes of all, since it can quickly turn from an administrative offense into a criminal one. The legal details deserve an article of their own: how the [blood alcohol limit on a boat](/en/blog/blood-alcohol-limit-boat/) is regulated, which thresholds case law applies, and what criminal consequences can follow under § 315a StGB are explained there in depth. What matters for this overview: on top of criminal proceedings, you also risk losing your license and your insurance coverage.

## Missing Safety Equipment and Overloading

Germany's navigation regulations prescribe a certain **minimum equipment**, depending on the type of boat and the waters, including life-saving appliances, appropriate day shapes, and functioning navigation lights. If this equipment is missing or defective, that is a classic case for an administrative offense during an inspection.

**Overloading** works similarly: every registered boat has a maximum number of persons or a maximum load it may carry. Exceeding this limit violates the boat's registration terms. Beyond the fine risk, there's a practical issue too: after an accident involving an overloaded or under-equipped boat, insurers scrutinize closely whether gross negligence reduced or excluded coverage. For more on what matters when choosing [boat insurance](/en/blog/boat-insurance-for-sportboats-what-makes-sense/), see our dedicated article.

## Violating Right-of-Way, Light, and Sound Signal Rules

Ignoring the [right-of-way rules](/en/blog/right-of-way-collision-rules/), overtaking incorrectly, failing to show the prescribed navigation lights, or giving sound signals incorrectly or not at all are, first and foremost, administrative offenses under the BinSchStrO, SeeSchStrO, or COLREGs. But these violations also matter after the fact: if you're shown to have violated a right-of-way rule or the lighting requirements, you frequently share liability when a resulting damage claim is settled under civil law, even if the other party also contributed to the accident.

## Speed Limits and Protected Zones

Many inland lakes and river sections have posted **speed limits**, for example near swimming areas, locks, or narrow fairways. On top of that, many waters include **protected zones** in nature reserves, where boating is restricted by time or area, or prohibited altogether, to protect breeding grounds for birds or sensitive shorelines. Overlooking or ignoring such signage is an administrative offense, no matter how well you otherwise handle your boat. On popular lakes, the water police and local authorities tend to check exactly these spots on busy weekends.

## Registration and Markings

Recreational boats above a certain engine power must carry an official registration marking and be registered with the responsible Wasserstraßen- und Schifffahrtsamt. A missing or illegible registration marking, or a sale that was never properly re-registered, is also a violation that shows up during an inspection. In practice, that means: if you buy a used boat, get the re-registration done promptly instead of letting it slide and being caught out later.

## Conduct After an Accident

Unlike road traffic, where leaving the scene of an accident is explicitly a criminal offense under § 142 StGB, there is no directly equivalent provision for waterway traffic. Clear duties apply nonetheless: under **§ 1.16 BinSchStrO**, everyone involved in a vessel accident must find out about the consequences of the accident and enable the identification of their person, their vessel, and the nature of their involvement, for the benefit of other parties and injured persons. If people are endangered by the accident, or if it threatens to block a waterway or lock, every nearby skipper is obliged to render assistance without delay, as far as this is compatible with the safety of their own vessel. Anyone who simply leaves without helping an injured person risks additional liability under **§ 323c StGB (failure to render assistance)**, a provision that applies generally, on the water just as much as on the road.

## Environmental Violations – the Most Expensive Mistake

The most serious violation, carrying the highest potential penalties, is **water pollution**. Anyone who unlawfully discharges oil, fuel, bilge water, or other contaminants into the water, whether during an engine change, a leak, or refueling, can be held liable under **§ 324 StGB**. This offense covers any unlawful pollution or adverse change to a body of water, covering both inland waters and the sea. Intentional conduct can carry **imprisonment of up to five years or a fine**; negligent conduct up to three years. Even an attempt is punishable. Only discharges covered by an official permit, for instance within a properly licensed operation, are exempt.

In practice, this means: bilge water with traces of oil does not go over the side, it belongs in the designated collection stations available in harbors and marinas. Anyone careless during refueling or maintenance work risks far more than a fine.

## Operating a Radio Without a Valid Certificate

Anyone operating a marine radio on board needs a valid radio operator's certificate, either UBI or SRC depending on the equipment. Operating a radio installation without the required authorization under German telecommunications law can also be penalized significantly. Which certificate you need for which boat and cruising area is explained in detail in the article on [UBI vs. SRC](/en/blog/ubi-vs-src-radio-license/).

## Consequences at a Glance

Taken together, a violation on the water can touch several levels at once:

- **Formal warning fine (Verwarngeld):** for minor violations handled informally.
- **Fine (Bußgeld):** following a formal administrative proceeding, with the amount depending on the individual case and the responsible authority.
- **Criminal proceedings:** for offenses such as § 315a StGB (intoxication), § 323c StGB (failure to render assistance), or § 324 StGB (water pollution), carrying a fine or imprisonment.
- **License consequences:** where there is justified doubt about someone's fitness, the responsible authority can revoke the Sportbootführerschein.
- **Insurance consequences:** gross negligence or intent can lead an insurer to reduce or fully deny coverage.

## How to Avoid Expensive Mistakes

Most of the violations covered here are easy to avoid with a bit of preparation:

- Keep your **license and documents** on board and ready, and make sure your license matches the waters you're on.
- Check your **safety equipment** regularly, especially life-saving appliances and day shapes.
- Know and respect your boat's **maximum number of persons** and load capacity.
- Know the **right-of-way, light, and sound signal rules** confidently, not just for the exam but for everyday boating.
- Respect posted **speed limits and protected zones**, even if other boats go faster.
- **Re-register** your boat's markings and registration promptly after a purchase or sale.
- **After an accident**, stay calm, render assistance, and allow your identification, instead of simply carrying on.
- Dispose of **oil, fuel, and bilge water** only at designated collection stations, never overboard.

## In the SBF Exam

Many of these topics, from the duty to carry your license to equipment requirements and conduct after an accident, are a regular part of the official ELWIS exam catalog for both the [SBF Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) and [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/) exams. Understanding the reasoning behind the rules, rather than just memorizing answers, makes the exam easier and makes you genuinely safer on the water afterward.

## Conclusion

There is no single fine catalog for recreational boats, but rather an interplay of navigation regulations, the license ordinance, and the criminal code. Smaller violations, such as missing equipment or right-of-way mistakes, usually end as administrative offenses with a warning or a fine. Alcohol at the helm, leaving the scene of an accident, or discharging oil and bilge water, on the other hand, can become genuine criminal offenses carrying a fine or imprisonment, on top of the risk of losing your license and your insurance coverage. Knowing and taking the rules seriously doesn't just make you a safer skipper, it can also save you a lot of money and trouble.
