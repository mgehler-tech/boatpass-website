A passage at night has a special appeal: calm water, a sky full of stars, and harbor lights reflecting on the surface. At the same time, darkness is more demanding than any daytime trip, because you have to rely almost entirely on lights, instruments, and your hearing. This article shows you how to properly prepare a night passage in a sportboat, keep your orientation, and which duties apply after dark.

## Why Night Passages Are More Demanding

During the day you judge distances, speeds, and the size of other vessels almost automatically. At night that experience falls away. You no longer see a boat, only its **lights**, and from those you have to work out what kind of vessel it is, where it is heading, and how far away it is. Distances over water are hard to estimate in the dark because familiar reference points are missing.

Other factors add to this: fatigue sets in faster after sunset, the temperature drops, and single bright lights ashore can dazzle you or distract you from the signals that matter. Knowing this, you plan a night passage more deliberately and with more margin than a daytime trip. The good news: with solid preparation and calm handling, a night passage is very manageable.

## Preparation: Boat, Equipment, and Crew

The most important rule is this: **everything important gets prepared in daylight.** Searching for the flashlight or a life jacket in the dark costs time and nerves. Run through a short checklist before you cast off:

- **Check the lights:** Do the masthead light, sidelights, and sternlight (or the all-round light) work? Are spare bulbs or a charged backup light on board?
- **Life jackets** for everyone on board, worn and correctly adjusted. Recovering a person who has gone overboard is much harder at night, which makes a life jacket especially important here.
- **A flashlight** (waterproof), and ideally a second one as a backup.
- **Navigation tools:** an up-to-date chart or chartplotter, a compass, and a VHF radio on longer trips. Plan your route in advance.
- **Clothing:** warm, waterproof layers, because it gets noticeably colder on the water at night than ashore.

Also talk through the **roles in the crew** beforehand: who steers, who keeps lookout, who works the radio if needed. Clear agreements save a lot of hectic shouting in the dark.

## Preserving Your Night Vision

Your eye is a surprisingly good night vision device, but only if you let it work. The switch from light to dark, known as **dark adaptation**, takes roughly **20 to 30 minutes**. During this time your eyes adjust to the darkness and you can make out far more detail. A single glance into a bright white light can destroy that adaptation instantly, and you start over from the beginning.

So on board at night:

- **No harsh white light** in the cockpit. Use **red or heavily dimmed light** for the chart and instruments, because it disturbs your night vision the least.
- **Do not look directly into light sources**, whether your own, those of other boats, or lights ashore.
- Set plotter and phone displays as dark as possible.

Protecting your night vision deliberately lets you spot faint lights and dark obstacles much earlier.

## Finding Your Way at Night

By day you orient yourself using coastlines, buildings, and buoys. At night those reference points disappear, but others come to the fore. **Lighthouses and lit sea marks** now become your most important markers. Many lights have their own **characteristic**, a fixed pattern of flashes, blinks, or eclipses, recorded on the chart. Through this characteristic you can identify a light unambiguously and fix your position.

The **buoyage** helps too: important buoys are lit and show colors you can assign to the lateral system. For how buoyage is structured in general, see our article on [sea marks and the lateral system](/en/blog/sea-marks-lateral-system-explained/). In addition, a **chartplotter with GPS** gives you a reliable position readout, but you always cross-check it against what you actually see outside. Never rely on a single device alone; combine sight, chart, compass, and plotter.

## Lights: Seeing and Being Seen

At night, others recognize you only through your **navigation lights**. The prescribed lights must be shown from **sunset to sunrise**, and also in restricted visibility (COLREGs Rule 20). Without lights you are effectively invisible to other vessels, and you also cannot signal where you are heading.

Just as important is reading other vessels' lights **correctly**: if a vessel shows both sidelights and a masthead light, it is coming toward you. If you see only a white sternlight, it is moving away. Which lights each vessel carries, and how to tell power-driven from sailing vessels, is explained in detail in our article on [navigation lights](/en/blog/navigation-lights-explained/). At night this knowledge is no longer an exam topic but directly practical: from the light combination you work out who has right of way and who must give way.

## Safe Speed and Lookout

Two basic duties of the collision regulations (COLREGs) matter especially at night. First, the **proper lookout** (COLREGs Rule 5): you must constantly observe what is happening around you by sight, hearing, and all available means. At night this also means deliberately listening for sounds, such as engines or signals you hear before you see them.

Second, **safe speed** (COLREGs Rule 6): only ever travel fast enough that you can stop or take avoiding action within the distance you can oversee. Because visibility is reduced at night, this usually means: **go slower than during the day.** A reduced speed gives you more time to interpret lights, spot obstacles, and react calmly. This is decisive especially in narrow channels, near harbors, and in heavy traffic.

## Your Duties and the Legal Framework

There is no general night boating ban for recreational craft. So you may in principle boat after sunset, but you have to follow the applicable rules. These include above all the prescribed **navigation lights**, **safe speed**, a proper **lookout**, and the principle of **good seamanship**, meaning anticipatory, considerate behavior.

Important: individual waters may have **local restrictions**, for example on certain inland waterways, in nature reserves, or in harbor areas. So inform yourself about the rules of your specific area before a night passage. For the basics of where your license actually lets you go, see our page on the [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/) license. Anyone who has the right-of-way rules down cold is clearly at an advantage at night too; read more in our article on [right of way and collision rules](/en/blog/right-of-way-collision-rules/).

## Tips for Your First Night Passage

If you are out in the dark for the first time, ease into it:

- **Start at dusk.** Casting off while there is still some light lets you get used to the growing darkness step by step, instead of heading straight into deep night.
- **Choose a familiar area.** A route you know well by day is much easier at night.
- **Keep the route simple** and allow generous time.
- **Do not go alone**, at least for the first few times. A second person for lookout takes a huge amount of pressure off.
- **Stay calm.** Most difficulties come from rushing. Reduce your speed and you almost always win back the time you need.

## Conclusion

A night passage in a sportboat is a special experience, but it demands more preparation and calm than a daytime trip. Four things are decisive: **careful preparation in daylight**, **protecting your night vision**, **clean orientation** using lights, sea marks, and instruments, and consistent attention to your duties under the COLREGs, namely navigation lights, safe speed, and lookout. If you start slowly and choose a familiar area, you quickly build the confidence for more passages after sunset.
