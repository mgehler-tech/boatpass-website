You're planning to use a VHF radio on board – and suddenly you're faced with two abbreviations: **UBI** and **SRC**. Both are radio certificates for recreational boaters, but they apply to entirely different bodies of water. This article explains which certificate is right for you, what each exam covers, how registration works, and why some routes call for both.

## What Is a Radio Certificate – and When Do You Need One?

A **VHF radio** is far more than a convenience on board. It is the central safety tool for sending distress calls, communicating with locks and bridges, and coordinating in harbours. In Germany, anyone who **personally operates** such a device needs a valid radio certificate.

One important point upfront: radio certificates are **not a prerequisite for the Sportbootführerschein** (the German recreational boat licence, or SBF). You can take the [SBF Binnen (inland) or SBF See (coastal)](/sbf-binnen-vs-see/) exam without holding a radio certificate. The radio licence is a separate document – required the moment you pick up the microphone yourself.

The German recreational radio system is split into two areas: **inland radio** for rivers, canals, and inland lakes, and **sea radio** for coastal waters and open sea. Both use VHF frequencies, but they rely on different channels, different identification systems, and different emergency procedures. That is why two separate certificates exist.

## UBI – The Certificate for Inland Waterways

**UBI** stands for **Ultrakurzwellen-Betriebszeugnis für den Binnenschifffahrtsfunk** (roughly: ultra-short-wave operating certificate for inland waterway radio). It authorises the operation of a VHF radio on European inland waterways.

### Where Is the UBI Valid?

The UBI is valid on **German and European inland waterways**: the Rhine, Danube, Moselle, Elbe, Weser, and associated canals and lakes classified as federal waterways. Neighbouring countries recognise the UBI on their inland waterways as well – for example on Dutch canals or the Belgian Meuse. It is the standard certificate for anyone who primarily navigates rivers, canals, and inland lakes.

### What Do You Learn for the UBI?

The UBI course and exam cover the following topics:

- **Operating a VHF radio** on inland waterways
- **ATIS system** (Automatic Transmitter Identification System): a digital identifier transmitted automatically after each call, making the vessel identifiable on inland waterways
- Key channels for inland use – including Channel 10 on the Rhine for ship-to-ship traffic, and Channel 16 as the international distress channel
- **Lock and bridge communication**: how to announce yourself correctly when approaching a lock
- Distress and safety procedures on inland waterways
- Radio etiquette and region-specific rules

The exam consists of a **written and a practical** component, administered by the German Federal Network Agency (Bundesnetzagentur) or accredited examiners, and offered through boating schools, associations such as DMYV or ADAC Motorboat clubs, and local sailing clubs.

### The ATIS System – What Is It?

The ATIS system is the backbone of inland waterway radio in Germany and much of Europe. Every inland VHF radio carries an **8-digit ATIS code** that is transmitted automatically at the end of each radio call. Other vessels and port authorities can instantly identify who transmitted – without you having to state your name explicitly.

Your ATIS code must be **registered with the Bundesnetzagentur** (the German Federal Network Agency) before you can legally operate the device. Registration is straightforward and is usually done alongside obtaining the UBI certificate itself. The ATIS code is assigned to the vessel, not to the operator – if you change boats, you need a new code for that vessel.

### Who Should Get the UBI?

The UBI is the right choice if you:

- Hold or are working towards the **SBF Binnen** (inland boat licence)
- Mainly navigate German rivers, canals, or inland lakes
- Want to communicate with locks, bridges, and other vessels on inland waterways
- Have no plans for coastal or open-sea voyages

## SRC – The Certificate for Sea and Coastal Waters

**SRC** stands for **Short Range Certificate**. It is the internationally recognised VHF maritime radio certificate, authorising the operation of VHF radio equipment at sea.

### Where Is the SRC Valid?

The SRC is valid on **shipping lanes and sea waters worldwide**. It is based on recommendations from the **ITU (International Telecommunication Union)** and is accepted in virtually all European and international coastal and sea waters. Whether you're heading for the Baltic Sea, the North Sea, Croatia, Greece, or the Atlantic coast – the SRC keeps you fully covered internationally.

### What Do You Learn for the SRC?

The SRC covers **GMDSS** (Global Maritime Distress and Safety System) for short-range coastal operations. Exam topics include:

- Principles of the worldwide maritime distress, safety, and communication system
- **DSC** (Digital Selective Calling) on Channel 70 – for digitally sending distress, urgency, and safety calls
- **MMSI number** (Maritime Mobile Service Identity): the unique nine-digit identifier assigned to your vessel or device for use at sea
- Key channels at sea – Channel 16 as the international voice distress and calling channel, Channel 70 for DSC, and area-specific working channels
- Communication with coast radio stations, ports, weather services, and other vessels
- **English radiotelephony phraseology**: the international standard at sea, tested in the practical exam
- Distress, urgency, and safety procedures according to GMDSS requirements

Like the UBI, the SRC exam has a **written and a practical** component. It is offered by boating schools, DSV (the German Sailing Association), SKJ, and other accredited organisations.

### MMSI and DSC – Your Digital Identity at Sea

The **MMSI number** is the maritime equivalent of the ATIS code used on inland waterways. It is nine digits long and uniquely identifies your vessel in the maritime radio network. With a **DSC controller** (built into any modern VHF sea radio), you can send a digital distress alert on Channel 70 that automatically transmits your MMSI, your GPS position (if a GPS is connected), and the nature of the emergency.

This is a significant safety advantage over a voice-only call: even if you are incapacitated after sending the alert, the rescue coordination centre already has your identity and position. A DSC-equipped radio with a connected GPS is therefore far more than a communications tool – it is a life-safety device.

Your MMSI must also be **registered with the Bundesnetzagentur** before use. German recreational vessels receive an MMSI beginning with „211" – Germany's country code for maritime radio. Registration is free and must be completed before the DSC controller is activated for the first time.

### Who Should Get the SRC?

The SRC is the right certificate if you:

- Hold or are working towards the **SBF See** (coastal boat licence)
- Sail or motor on coastal waters – the North Sea, Baltic Sea, or abroad
- Navigate international waters where English is the standard radio language
- Want to use harbour radio, weather broadcasts, or coastal traffic services at sea

## The Key Differences: UBI vs. SRC

The fundamental question is always: **where are you sailing?** Inland or sea – your cruising area determines your certificate.

**ATIS vs. DSC:** Inland waterways use the **ATIS system** for automatic vessel identification. At sea, the standard is **DSC** (Digital Selective Calling) with a unique MMSI number. The equipment and procedures differ significantly – which is precisely why there are two separate licences.

**Language:** On inland waterways you communicate in German (or the national language of the country you're in). At sea, **English** is the international mandatory language and is tested in the SRC practical exam.

**Distress procedures:** Both systems have distress procedures, but the technology and workflow are different. GMDSS is a globally standardised system for sea use. Inland radio has its own, region- and country-specific rules.

**Recognition:** The SRC is internationally recognised for sea use. The UBI is valid on European inland waterways – but not at sea.

## Can I Hold Both Certificates?

Yes – and for many boaters this makes perfect sense. If you travel, for example, from a German river through the Kiel Canal (Nord-Ostsee-Kanal) into the Baltic Sea, you pass through both inland waterways and sea waters. Technically, you need both certificates if you want to operate the radio across the full route yourself.

The good news: both exams are well worth preparing for, and there is genuine overlap in foundational knowledge. Completing the UBI first gives you a solid grounding in radio etiquette and basic procedures before you move on to DSC and GMDSS for the SRC.

### Practical Tips for Exam Preparation

**For the UBI:**
- Get a current study guide covering the inland channel plan and ATIS procedures.
- Practise the practical component carefully – the examiner will test whether you can operate a radio device confidently and state the correct call sequence.
- Learn the region-specific channels for the major waterways (Rhine, Moselle, Danube) – these frequently appear in the exam.

**For the SRC:**
- English phraseology is its own study area. Practise the standard formulae out loud – this makes the spoken part of the practical exam far less stressful.
- Learn the DSC distress procedure step by step: initiating the alert, acknowledging a received alert, and switching to Channel 16 for voice communication.
- Many SRC courses include hands-on practice with real equipment. This time at the radio is especially valuable for the practical exam.

## Do I Need a Radio Certificate Just to Have a Radio on Board?

**Owning** and **carrying** a radio device on board is not tied to a certificate. The licence is required as soon as you **personally operate** the device. Another person on board who holds a valid certificate may operate it, as long as you don't touch the controls yourself.

However, there is an important exception for **emergencies**: in a genuine distress situation, anyone on board may operate the radio to call for help – even without a certificate. That said, holding a certificate and knowing the correct procedures in advance remains your responsibility and could save critical minutes at sea. Someone who has to figure out how to send a distress call under pressure is at a serious disadvantage compared to someone who has practised it.

## What Comes After UBI and SRC?

The SRC covers **short-range** operations – specifically coastal waters where VHF range is sufficient. The range of a VHF radio depends on antenna height and terrain, but typically extends to a few dozen nautical miles. If you're planning offshore passages or transatlantic voyages beyond VHF range, you'll need the **LRC (Long Range Certificate)** – the long-range maritime radio certificate covering HF radio and satellite communication systems. For most recreational boaters in German and European waters, however, the SRC is more than adequate.

## Conclusion

**UBI** is your licence for inland waterways; **SRC** is your licence for the sea. They use different identification systems (ATIS vs. DSC/MMSI), different distress procedures, and are recognised in different areas. Choose based on your cruising ground – and if you sail both inland and offshore, it is well worth holding both certificates.
