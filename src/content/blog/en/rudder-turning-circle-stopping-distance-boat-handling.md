A car steers with its front wheels, brakes reliably on a dry road and stays exactly where you park it. A boat does none of that. It steers with its stern, keeps gliding after you take off the throttle, gets pushed around by wind and current and turns around a point that shifts depending on how it is moving. Once you understand these quirks, you handle a boat more calmly, plan maneuvers ahead and find the practical exam for the German recreational boating license (**Sportbootführerschein**, SBF) much easier. This article explains how **rudder angle, turning circle and stopping distance** fit together and what that means for you at the helm.

## Why a boat behaves differently from a car

The key difference: a boat has **no fixed contact with the ground**. It floats in a medium that may itself be moving, while the wind acts on it at the same time. That leads to three principles worth keeping in mind for every maneuver:

- **Steering needs water flow.** A rudder or a steerable drive only works when water flows past it or the propeller produces thrust.
- **A boat steers with its stern.** The rudder sits at the back, so the stern is pushed sideways and the bow turns in the desired direction.
- **A boat has no brakes.** All you have is coasting and reverse thrust. The distance to a standstill is always longer than beginners expect.

These principles apply to a small motorboat on a lake just as much as to a sailing yacht under engine. How strong each effect is varies by boat type, but the basic behavior stays the same.

## Understanding rudder angle and rudder effect

**Rudder angle** describes how far and to which side the rudder is turned. Amidships means straight ahead; rudder to port or starboard means the bow turns that way. How strongly the boat responds depends on the **rudder effect**.

### Speed through the water is what counts

A rudder blade creates a sideways force because the passing water is deflected by it. The faster the water flows, the stronger the effect. This explains a lot of harbor maneuvering: **at very low speed the boat responds sluggishly**, and when stationary it does not respond at all. What matters is speed **through the water**, not over ground. If you motor slowly against a current, the boat may hardly move over ground yet still steer well.

### Propeller wash as your secret weapon

On boats with a fixed rudder blade directly behind the propeller there is a useful trick: a **short, firm burst of forward thrust with the rudder over** directs the propeller wash straight onto the rudder blade. The stern moves sideways before the boat gathers much speed. This lets you turn a boat in a tight space without using much room ahead.

In reverse this effect is largely missing, because the propeller pushes water forward, away from the rudder. Going astern, the rudder only starts to work once the boat has noticeable speed through the water.

### Outboards and sterndrives

**Outboard motors and sterndrives** have no separate rudder blade. You steer by swiveling the entire drive and thereby changing the direction of thrust. The consequence: **no thrust, hardly any steering.** Anyone who drifts toward a jetty in neutral while spinning the wheel notices this straight away. On the other hand, with the drive turned and short bursts of throttle you can maneuver very precisely, both ahead and astern.

## Pivot point and turning circle

When you put the rudder over, a boat does not turn around its bow the way a car turns around its front axle. It rotates around an imaginary **pivot point** whose position depends on the direction of travel.

### Where is the pivot point?

As a rule of thumb:

- **Moving ahead**, the pivot point lies in the forward part of the hull.
- **Going astern**, it moves aft.
- **When stationary**, it lies roughly in the middle of the boat.

Its exact position depends on hull shape, weight distribution and speed. For practical purposes, the principle is enough.

### The stern swings out

Because the boat pivots near the front when moving ahead, **the stern swings toward the outside of the turn**. That is why you should not pass close to a jetty or a moored boat and turn away only at the last moment: the bow clears, but the stern swings toward the obstacle. Always leave enough room to the side for the stern swing when you turn away.

When **leaving a berth** you can use this effect deliberately. If the boat is lying alongside a jetty, simply motoring ahead with the rudder over often fails, because the stern pushes into the jetty. It is frequently better to get the stern clear first, for example with the help of a spring line or by briefly going astern. ### The turning circle

The **turning circle** is the circle a boat describes with full rudder. Its size depends on factors such as:

- **Boat length and hull shape:** long, narrow hulls with a long keel usually need more room than short boats.
- **Drive and rudder:** steerable drives, large rudder blades or a bow thruster make the turning circle smaller.
- **Speed:** at higher speed the boat carries more momentum and slides outward in the turn.
- **Wind and current:** they distort the circle because the boat is set sideways during the turn.

The only way to get reliable figures for your particular boat is to try it out, and that is well worth doing. On a calm day in open water, make a full circle to port and one to starboard and watch how much room you need. The two directions often differ, which is linked to prop walk.

## Prop walk

A propeller does not just produce thrust, it also creates a **sideways force**. This **prop walk** (in German, *Radeffekt*, the "wheel effect") pushes the stern sideways, as if the propeller were rolling along the bottom like a wheel.

What matters is the propeller's direction of rotation. With a **right-handed propeller**, which turns clockwise when viewed from astern while going ahead, the following typically applies:

- **Going astern**, the stern is pulled to **port**. This is usually where the effect is most noticeable.
- **Going ahead**, the force acts toward the other side, but it is largely compensated by the good rudder effect.

With a left-handed propeller it is the other way round. On boats with a rudder blade, prop walk is often more noticeable than on outboards, which partly compensate through the direction of thrust. Find out which way your boat pulls in reverse. The easiest way is to engage reverse in open water with the rudder amidships and watch where the stern goes.

Prop walk is not a defect but a tool. If you know the stern pulls to port in reverse, you prefer to come alongside port side to, because a short burst astern to stop the boat then pulls the stern toward the jetty. Experienced skippers also use the effect deliberately when turning in a tight space.

## Stopping distance and stopping the boat

A boat has no brakes. This is the point beginners underestimate most often.

### Letting it coast

When you take off the throttle, water resistance gradually slows the boat down. How quickly that happens depends heavily on the type of boat:

- **Planing boats** drop off the plane when you throttle back and lose a lot of speed in the process. Watch out for your own stern wave, which can catch up from behind and give the boat a brief push.
- **Displacement boats**, meaning heavier boats that always sit in the water, keep their way on much longer. Heavy sailing yachts under engine in particular often glide surprisingly far.

The **heavier** a boat, the more momentum it carries. That applies to the same boat too: fully loaded with crew, luggage and full tanks it handles more sluggishly than when you are out alone.

### Stopping with reverse

If coasting is not enough, you **stop the boat** actively: throttle off, a short pause in neutral, then engage reverse and apply throttle gradually. The pause in neutral is kinder to the gearbox and drive. Full throttle astern from high speed is not an everyday maneuver, at most an emergency measure. Remember prop walk here too: when you stop with reverse thrust, the stern swings to one side.

### What stopping distance depends on

There is no single figure for how far you travel before coming to a standstill. It depends on **speed, weight, hull shape, engine power, wind and current**. Going with the current you are faster over ground and need more distance; against the current you stop sooner. So always plan generously, and get to know how your boat stops in open water before you need it in a tight harbor.

## Wind, current and shallow water

Besides rudder and propeller, external forces act on the boat. At low speed they become more and more significant compared with the rudder effect.

- **Wind:** the bow, superstructure, cabin or bimini top all offer windage. At low speed, on many motorboats the bow tends to be blown off downwind, because there is a lot of area but little draft at the front. How strong this is depends on the boat.
- **Current:** it sets the whole boat. On rivers it often makes sense to come alongside heading into the current, because you can then move slowly over ground and still steer.
- **Shallow water:** when there is little water under the keel, the boat can respond more sluggishly and the hull can sit deeper. In shallow stretches, for example on canals, keep your speed appropriately low.

For all three the same rule applies: **assess them before the maneuver, not during it.** A quick look at flags, the water surface and floating objects tells you what to expect.

## What this means for the practical exam

In the practical exam for the [SBF Inland](/en/sbf-inland/) and [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/) licenses you will be asked to perform maneuvers such as leaving a berth, coming alongside, turning in a confined space and the [man overboard maneuver](/en/blog/man-overboard-maneuver/). All of them depend directly on how well you judge rudder effect, pivot point, prop walk and stopping distance. You can find the exact exam procedure on the [SBF exam](/en/sbf-exam/) page.

Examiners mainly look for a **calm, forward-looking approach**. Typical moments where candidates start to sweat:

- **Too much speed when coming alongside**, because the coasting distance was underestimated.
- **Frantic wheel-spinning** at low speed, even though there is no rudder effect.
- **Forgetting the stern swing**, so the stern gets too close to an obstacle when turning away.
- **Being surprised by prop walk** when going astern.

Once you have internalized the principles in this article, you can anticipate these situations instead of merely reacting to them.

## How to get to know your boat

Every boat has its own character. A short practice routine in open water helps you learn it quickly:

1. **Make full circles** to port and starboard, slowly and at medium speed. Compare how much room you need.
2. **Practice stopping** from different speeds. Pick a mental reference point and check how far you travel.
3. **Engage reverse** with the rudder amidships and watch which way the stern pulls.
4. **Test slow speed:** at what speed does the boat still respond reliably to the rudder?
5. **Let it drift:** put the engine in neutral and watch how wind and current set the boat and which way the bow turns.

Repeat these exercises whenever you take over a different boat, such as a charter boat. What else matters when taking over a boat is covered in [Boat Handover Check Before Your First Trip](/en/blog/boat-handover-check-charter-or-own-boat/).

## Conclusion

A boat responds differently from a car: it steers with its stern, needs water flowing past the rudder or thrust from the drive, turns around a shifting pivot point and has no brakes. On top of that come prop walk and the forces of wind, current and shallow water. If you understand how these fit together, you plan maneuvers with enough room, go slowly but not too slowly, and use prop walk and propeller wash to your advantage. Most important of all is practice in open water before things get tight in the harbor.
