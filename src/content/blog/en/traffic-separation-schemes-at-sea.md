Off busy coasts and in narrow passages, ship traffic is organized so that large vessels do not meet on opposing courses. The tool for this is the **traffic separation scheme**, in simple terms the highway at sea. For recreational boaters, it is important to know how such a scheme is laid out and which rules apply, because mistakes here are dangerous and can get expensive. This article explains the basics and the correct behavior under COLREGs Rule 10.

## What Is a Traffic Separation Scheme?

A **traffic separation scheme (TSS)** guides opposing traffic flows into separate lanes. It consists of several elements:

- **Traffic lanes:** one-way lanes in which traffic runs in a single direction, like the two directional carriageways of a highway.
- **Separation zone or line:** the area in the middle that separates the opposing lanes. It should generally be kept clear.
- **Inshore traffic zone:** the area between the landward boundary of the TSS and the coast, intended for small craft and local traffic.

Traffic separation schemes are marked on the **nautical chart** and, in busy areas, are often monitored from shore stations. For how to read such chart symbols, see our article on [reading a nautical chart](/en/blog/how-to-read-a-nautical-chart/).

## Why This Matters for Recreational Boaters

Even though traffic separation schemes are primarily meant for commercial shipping, recreational boats regularly pass by or through them, for example on coastal trips on the North and Baltic Seas or when transiting narrow areas. The lanes carry large, fast ships with long distances needed to give way or stop. A recreational boat that behaves incorrectly there puts itself in serious danger. That is why the correct behavior is a fixed part of the [SBF Coastal](/en/sbf-coastal/) theory and of the collision regulations.

## The Key Rules (COLREGs Rule 10)

Behavior in and near traffic separation schemes is governed by **Rule 10 of the collision regulations (COLREGs)**. The most important points for recreational boaters:

- **Go with the flow:** if you use a lane, travel in the **general direction of traffic** for that lane.
- **Keep the separation zone clear:** the separation zone or line must generally **not** be entered or crossed, except when crossing or when joining or leaving.
- **Join and leave at the ends:** normally you enter or leave at the **ends** of a lane. If you have to join from the side, do so at **as small an angle as possible** to the direction of traffic.
- **Use the inshore traffic zone:** vessels under **20 meters** in length, sailing vessels, and fishing vessels may and should use the **inshore traffic zone** instead of entering a lane.
- **Do not impede:** a vessel under 20 meters in length or a sailing vessel must **not impede the safe passage of a power-driven vessel** in the lane.
- **Extra caution at the ends:** proceed with particular care at the **ends** of the lanes, where traffic joins and leaves.
- **Avoid anchoring:** as far as possible, **do not anchor** within a traffic separation scheme or near its ends.

## Crossing: The Right Way

The most important point, and the one asked most often in the exam, is **crossing**. The principle: cross a traffic separation scheme **as little as possible**. If it cannot be avoided, there is a clear requirement: you cross the lane with your **heading (keel line) at right angles** to the general direction of traffic.

The detail matters: the right angle refers to the **orientation of the boat (keel line)**, not to the course over ground. Even if current or wind set you sideways, you keep the boat crosswise to the direction of traffic. This makes you **predictable** for commercial shipping, clearly shows that you are crossing rather than travelling along, and gets you out of the danger zone by the shortest route. Cross promptly and with sufficient speed, but wait to start until there is a safe gap in the traffic.

Note: crossing at right angles does not release you from the other [right-of-way rules](/en/blog/right-of-way-collision-rules/) or from the principle of good seamanship. As a small vessel, you must not impede large ships in the lane.

## Practical Tips for Recreational Boaters

- **Go around if you can:** use the inshore traffic zone or plan your trip so that you do not have to cross the scheme at all.
- **Plan in advance:** draw the crossing on the chart beforehand and decide where and in which direction you cross.
- **Watch the traffic:** keep an eye on large ships early. They are faster than they look and cannot give way like a recreational boat. A VHF radio and, where available, AIS help you assess the situation.
- **Decide clearly:** hesitant back-and-forth confuses others. Wait for a gap, then cross decisively.

## Conclusion

Traffic separation schemes organize the traffic of large ships and are relevant to recreational boaters mainly when crossing. The core rules from **COLREGs Rule 10**: travel with the direction of traffic, keep the separation zone clear, use the inshore traffic zone, do not impede large ships in the lane, and when necessary cross promptly with your **heading at right angles**. Anyone who has these points down and watches the traffic ahead moves safely even in busy areas.
