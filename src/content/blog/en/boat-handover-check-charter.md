Your holiday starts, the charter boat is waiting at the dock, and you would love to cast off right away. This is exactly where most avoidable mistakes happen. Half an hour of thorough handover checks protects you from nasty surprises on the water and from disputes over the deposit when you return the boat. This article walks you step by step through what to look for when taking over a charter boat, and what also applies when you take a borrowed or newly bought boat out for the first time.

## Why the Handover Check Matters

The handover establishes the condition in which you receive the boat. Anything not recorded there may, in case of doubt, be attributed to you. The handover check therefore has two goals:

- **Safety:** you want to know that the engine, steering, and safety equipment work before you are on the water.
- **Protection:** you want to document existing damage and missing inventory so it is not charged to you later.

Do not let yourself be rushed, even if the dock is busy. A reputable charter company takes time for the handover.

## On Site: Paperwork and Contract

Start with the paperwork, so your mind is free for the boat itself:

- **Charter contract:** do the boat, dates, area, and agreed extras match?
- **Boat documents:** which papers belong on board, and where are they kept?
- **License:** do you have the right license for the boat and the area with you? For many charter boats you need a Sportbootführerschein; on certain inland areas a [charter certificate](/en/blog/charter-certificate-rent-boat-without-license/) is enough.
- **Deposit and insurance:** how high is the deposit, what does the charter company's insurance cover, and what is the excess? For questions about insurance cover, see our article on [boat insurance](/en/blog/boat-insurance-for-sportboats-what-makes-sense/).

## Checking Hull, Deck, and Fittings

Walk slowly around the boat once and across the deck. Look out for:

- **Scratches, dents, and cracks** in the hull, especially at the bow, the sides, and the stern.
- **Rubbing strake, stanchions, and fittings:** is everything secure, is anything bent?
- **Fenders and mooring lines:** are there enough, and are they in good condition?
- **Anchor and chain:** are both there, and does the windlass work, if fitted?
- **Cushions, covers, and bimini:** tears and stains belong in the report too.

**Photograph every piece of damage**, ideally with a time stamp and both a close-up and an overview shot. It takes a few minutes and is your best evidence at return.

## Engine, Systems, and Fluid Levels

Have the **charter company show you** the systems rather than exploring them underway:

- **Start the engine** and let it run briefly. Is cooling water coming out where it should? Any unusual noises or warning lights?
- **Gear shift and steering:** forward, reverse, rudder travel in both directions.
- **Levels:** fuel, fresh water, and holding tank if applicable. Note the levels, since the boat often has to be returned with the same amount of fuel.
- **Electrics:** batteries, main switch, navigation lights, bilge pump, and instruments.
- **Marine toilet and pumps:** have the operation explained; a wrongly operated toilet is a classic cause of expensive repairs.

Ask explicitly about the **boat's quirks**, such as an engine that pulls to one side in reverse or one that only starts in a certain sequence.

## Is the Safety Equipment Complete?

You check the safety equipment not for the charter company, but for yourself:

- **Life jackets** in sufficient number and the right sizes, including for children. Which buoyancy class makes sense is explained in our article on [life jackets](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/).
- **Lifebuoy or throwing line** within reach.
- **Fire extinguisher:** present, accessible, and not overdue for inspection.
- **First aid kit**, flashlight, and horn.
- **Paddle or boat hook** for emergencies and docking.

Memorize **where** everything is. In an emergency you should not have to search.

## Area Briefing

Many charter companies offer a short **area briefing**. Use it. Ask about shallows, bridge clearances, lock times, restricted areas, speed limits, and good places to moor. Local knowledge does not replace a chart, but it adds a great deal to it.

## The Handover Report

At the end comes the **handover report**. It records the condition at takeover: known damage, inventory, fluid levels, and peculiarities. Read it carefully, **add everything you found yourself**, and only then sign. Get a copy or photograph the signed report. If damage occurs during the charter, report it to the charter company as soon as possible instead of only raising it at return.

## Terms to Clarify in Advance

Besides the boat's condition, the charter contract often covers points that can cost money at return. It is best to clarify them at takeover:

- **Fuel billing:** some charter companies require a full tank or the same fuel level as at takeover, others bill by **engine hours**, for example with houseboats. So note the engine hour meter reading at takeover.
- **Return time:** by when must the boat be back, and what happens if you are late?
- **Final cleaning:** is it included in the price, charged as a flat fee, or do you have to clean yourself?
- **Deposit refund:** when and how do you get the deposit back, and what happens if damage is disputed?

What you know in advance cannot surprise you at return.

## Returning the Boat: Check-out Without Disputes

Returning the boat mirrors the takeover. Allow enough time:

- **Refuel**, if agreed, and tidy the boat.
- **Walk around the boat together** with the charter company and compare against the handover report.
- **Return the complete inventory.**
- If there are discrepancies, calmly refer to your photos from day one.

If you were thorough at takeover, you usually have nothing to fear at return.

## What About Your Own or a Borrowed Boat?

Even when no charter company is standing next to you, the same look is worthwhile before the first trip with a **borrowed, newly bought, or long-unused boat**: engine, steering, fluid levels, lights, and safety equipment. With a friend's boat, also clarify insurance and liability beforehand. ## Conclusion

A thorough handover check often takes no more than half an hour and pays off twice: you set off in a boat whose systems and safety equipment you know, and you are protected against unjustified claims at return. The key points: clarify the paperwork, photograph damage, have the systems shown to you, check the safety equipment, add to the report, and only then sign.
