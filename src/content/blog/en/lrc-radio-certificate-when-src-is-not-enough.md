You have your SRC, the VHF radio sits by the chart table and everything works well along the coast. Then the question comes up: what if you eventually want to go further, to Scandinavia, across the Bay of Biscay or even over the Atlantic? At that point you have to ask whether the **SRC** (Short Range Certificate) is still enough or whether you need the **LRC** (Long Range Certificate). This article explains where the line is, what the LRC covers and who the extra effort is really worth it for. It refers to the German system, where the certificates are issued through the DSV and DMYV.

## The short version: SRC and LRC

Both certificates belong to the **GMDSS** (Global Maritime Distress and Safety System), the worldwide maritime distress system. They differ in which radio services you are qualified to operate:

- The [**SRC**](/en/src/) qualifies you for VHF marine radio including DSC in coastal waters, sea area A1.
- The [**LRC**](/en/lrc/), in German the "Allgemeines Funkbetriebszeugnis", additionally covers MF and HF radio as well as satellite communication. It is valid for all sea areas A1 to A4.

That makes the LRC the highest marine radio certificate in recreational boating. It fully includes the scope of the SRC. Both are examined by the examination boards of the German Sailing Association (DSV) and the German Motor Yacht Association (DMYV), are internationally recognized and have no expiry date.

If you first need an overview of the basics, read [Marine Radio Basics for Beginners](/en/blog/marine-radio-basics-beginners/). For the comparison with the inland certificate, see [UBI vs. SRC](/en/blog/ubi-vs-src-radio-license/).

## Sea areas A1 to A4

The line between SRC and LRC is easiest to understand through the four GMDSS sea areas. They are defined by the kind of radio coverage, not by fixed nautical-mile lines:

- **A1:** The area within range of at least one VHF coast station with continuous DSC distress alerting. This is the near-coastal zone.
- **A2:** The area outside A1 but within range of at least one MF coast station with DSC alerting.
- **A3:** The area outside A1 and A2 but within the coverage of geostationary satellites (Inmarsat). This includes large parts of the oceans, but not the polar regions.
- **A4:** The remaining areas, essentially the polar regions.

The sea areas describe how a distress call can be sent and received. Where exactly A1 ends on a given coast depends on the equipment of the coast stations. VHF range is limited by nature, because VHF waves travel roughly in a straight line. Antenna height and the other station decide how far you get.

## Where the SRC reaches its limit

The SRC covers VHF radio. Once you move so far from the coast that you can no longer reliably reach coast stations or other vessels on VHF, you lack the tools intended for that situation: MF, HF and satellite communication. The SRC is not designed to qualify you to operate those.

In practice the LRC becomes relevant if you:

- plan **offshore or bluewater passages** where you are permanently outside VHF range (sea areas A2 to A4)
- want to use **MF or HF radio** on board, for example to receive weather reports or talk to other yachts
- want to operate **satellite communication within the GMDSS framework**, such as Inmarsat
- are planning a **long voyage, an Atlantic crossing or a circumnavigation**
- want a **comprehensive, worldwide marine radio certificate** instead of upgrading later

For most recreational boaters in Germany, everyday boating looks different: North Sea and Baltic Sea, coastal trips, inland waters and holidays in the Mediterranean. For that the SRC is the right certificate. If you are sure you will never go beyond the coastal zone, you can skip the LRC.

One legal detail you should always clarify separately: which radio equipment and which proof of qualification are required for your specific vessel and cruising area depends on the flag state, the area and the charter company. When in doubt, ask the charterer or the responsible authorities.

## What the LRC adds

The LRC goes well beyond the SRC. The official question catalog contains **76 questions** and covers, among others, these topic blocks:

- **VHF and operating procedures:** marine radio, DSC, voice procedures and GMDSS overview
- **MF and HF:** MF/HF radio, NAVTEX and transmission methods
- **Satellite radio:** GMDSS satellite services, Inmarsat, EPIRB and SART
- **Distress and safety traffic:** Mayday, distress DSC and coordination of search and rescue
- **Radio regulations:** ITU Radio Regulations, sea areas A1 to A4 and certification
- **English and phonetics:** distress English, the spelling alphabet and distress calls

The satellite topics and the interplay of the different radio services are new if you have only known VHF so far. You learn how a distress alert can be sent through different channels, and why several means of alerting are meant to be available on board in case one fails.

## How the LRC exam works

The structure of the exam is manageable, but not trivial:

- **Theory:** A questionnaire with **14 LRC questions** in **20 minutes**. You pass with **11 correct answers**.
- **SRC questionnaire:** If you do not yet hold an SRC, you also complete the SRC questionnaire with 24 questions. So you do not have to obtain the SRC separately beforehand.
- **English:** As with the SRC, there is an English part with dictation and translation, because international marine radio is conducted in English.
- **Practice:** A practical part on GMDSS equipment, where correct operation and the right order of steps matter.

You can apply from the age of **18**, and on the exam day you must be at least 17 years and 9 months old. A preparation course is not mandatory, although some radio schools offer one. The official exam fee varies by association and federal state and is roughly in the range of 188 to 207 euros. An optional course comes on top. Always check current fees directly with the responsible body, as they can change.

## LRC straight away or SRC first?

A common question is whether you should take the SRC first and the LRC later. Both work:

- **SRC first, then LRC:** Sensible if you are currently mostly cruising near the coast and long-distance sailing is still a future plan. You learn the VHF basics at your own pace and build on them later.
- **LRC straight away:** Sensible if your passage is firmly planned or you want to avoid two exam dates. The LRC exam includes the SRC material if you do not hold an SRC yet.

The LRC also helps with inland radio. Anyone who already holds a marine radio certificate such as the SRC or the more extensive LRC takes a shortened UBI exam. If you also want to use radio on inland waters, that order pays off.

## Typical mistakes and misunderstandings

**"My VHF set works everywhere."** VHF is limited to roughly line of sight. On the open sea the distance to the nearest coast station is often too great. That is why the other radio services exist.

**"The LRC automatically makes me a better skipper."** The certificate is proof of radio qualification. Navigation, seamanship and suitable equipment remain separate matters. If you plan offshore passages, you will hardly get around the [Sportküstenschifferschein (SKS)](/en/blog/sks-coastal-skippers-license/) and a lot of practice.

**"I can't take the LRC without an SRC."** You can. The exam is structured so that the SRC part is integrated.

**"I'll pick it up on the side."** The catalog is smaller than the SRC catalog, but the topics are technically demanding. Satellite procedures and radio regulations take time. Plan your preparation deliberately.

## Is the LRC worth it for you?

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **Where will I really be sailing in the next few years?** Only the North Sea, Baltic or Mediterranean: the SRC is enough. Offshore passages or the Atlantic: plan for the LRC.
2. **Will I operate MF, HF or satellite radio?** If so, you need the knowledge behind it.
3. **How much effort is it worth to me?** The certificate costs money and study time. Both pay off if you use it.

An honest look at your own plans saves time and money. And if your goals grow, you can take the LRC at any time, since it has no expiry date.

## Conclusion

The **SRC** is enough for VHF radio in coastal waters and therefore for most recreational boaters in Germany. The **LRC** becomes relevant once you want to communicate beyond VHF range using MF, HF or satellite, for example on a long voyage, an Atlantic crossing or a circumnavigation. It includes the SRC, covers all sea areas A1 to A4 and never expires. The exam consists of theory, English and a practical part on GMDSS equipment.
