A sunny day on the water, a cool drink in hand – it sounds harmless enough. But if you are the one at the helm, the legal picture in Germany is more serious than many recreational boaters realize. The alcohol rules for boat operators differ in some details from road traffic law, but the idea that "anything goes on the water" is a dangerous myth that can turn into a criminal offense. This article explains the actual rules, the thresholds you need to know, and why this topic appears in the official Sportbootführerschein (SBF) exam.

The Sportbootführerschein (SBF) is the German recreational boat license. It comes in two versions: SBF Binnen (inland waters) and SBF See (coastal waters). Both are required by law for motorboats above 11.03 kW (15 hp) on German federal waterways.

## The Legal Basis: More Than a Footnote

On German federal waterways, two core regulations govern alcohol behind the helm:

- The **Binnenschifffahrtsstraßen-Ordnung (BinSchStrO)** for inland waterways
- The **Seeschifffahrtsstraßen-Ordnung (SeeSchStrO)** for coastal waters

Both prohibit a vessel operator from commanding a boat while their ability to do so safely is impaired. Beyond these administrative regulations, **§ 316 of the German Criminal Code (StGB)** – Trunkenheit im Verkehr (drunk driving in traffic) – applies to all vehicles, including boats. Operating a boat while unfit due to alcohol is a criminal offense, regardless of whether an accident occurs.

This is not theoretical. The Wasserschutzpolizei (water police) conducts regular checks on German waterways, especially on busy summer weekends.

## The Key Thresholds: 1.0‰ and 1.6‰

Unlike road traffic law, there is no standalone fine system for boat operators at 0.5‰ (the way Germany's road traffic code works for car drivers). Instead, two blood alcohol concentration (BAC) thresholds matter for recreational boat operators:

**1.0‰ BAC – relative incapacity (relative Fahruntüchtigkeit):**
Above this level, German courts generally presume that the boat operator can no longer safely control the vessel, even without visible signs of impairment. This threshold is established by case law for watercraft and is covered in the official **ELWIS question catalogue** used for the SBF exam.

**1.6‰ BAC – absolute incapacity (absolute Fahruntüchtigkeit):**
At this level, incapacity is legally irrefutable. No counter-evidence is accepted. This threshold applies equally on the road and on the water. Operating a boat at 1.6‰ or above is always a criminal act, full stop.

Summary:

- **Below 1.0‰:** Fitness is presumed, but if impairment is visible, prosecution is still possible.
- **From 1.0‰:** Courts presume incapacity (relative unfitness).
- **From 1.6‰:** Absolute incapacity – no rebuttal possible, always a criminal offense.

**Note:** These thresholds reflect established German case law and SBF teaching standards. Since regulations can change, it is worth consulting current sources from DMYV or DSV before any voyage.

## The Grey Zone: Below 1.0‰ Is Not a Free Pass

Many boaters assume that staying below 1.0‰ makes them legally safe. That is only partially true.

**Relative incapacity** means that even below 1.0‰, prosecution is possible if **concrete signs of impairment** are present. Unsteady footing, slurred speech, difficulty docking, erratic course-keeping – all of these are evidence that water police officers can record.

If an operator shows visible signs of intoxication during a check, a BAC of 0.7 or 0.8‰ can still trigger criminal proceedings. There is no equivalent of the road traffic "0.5–1.6‰ as just a fine" bracket for boats. Observable signs of alcohol influence can be used as proof of relative incapacity at any BAC level.

## Fine or Criminal Offense?

The legal consequence depends on the BAC and the circumstances:

**Below 1.6‰ with visible impairment:**
Prosecution for relative incapacity under § 316 StGB is possible. Depending on severity and whether others were endangered, this can be treated as a minor administrative offense or a criminal matter. If an accident occurs under alcohol influence – even below 1.0‰ – additional charges such as negligent bodily injury may apply.

**At 1.6‰ or above (absolute incapacity):**
This is always a criminal offense under § 316 StGB, even without any observable impairment and without an accident. Potential consequences include a fine or custodial sentence, a criminal record entry, and in some cases loss of the driving licence for motor vehicles. How this affects the SBF itself depends on the specifics of the case.

There is also an insurance dimension: charter contracts and boat liability policies frequently include clauses that void coverage in cases of alcohol violations. A collision under alcohol influence can therefore result not only in criminal liability but also in full civil liability for damages.

## Inland and Coastal: Do the Same Rules Apply?

Yes. A common misunderstanding is that open-sea conditions come with looser alcohol rules. They do not.

The SeeSchStrO applies to German coastal waters and contains the same fundamental prohibition for impaired operators. Someone with the **SBF See** sailing in the Baltic or the North Sea is bound by the same rules as someone with the **SBF Binnen** on the Rhine or Lake Constance.

On the open sea, the **Bundespolizei See** (Federal Sea Police) conducts checks in addition to the Wasserschutzpolizei. In international waters, the COLREGs (International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea) also require vessels to be under safe command at all times.

## Sailing Boats: The Rules Still Apply

Another misconception: sailing without the engine running puts you outside alcohol law. It does not.

Sailing vessels are watercraft under German maritime law. A skipper on a sailing boat under sail – with the engine off – is bound by the same alcohol rules. Operating a sailing boat while intoxicated can lead to prosecution under § 316 StGB just as it would for a motorboat operator.

## Passengers and Crew

Passengers who are not operating the vessel are not directly subject to the alcohol prohibition. There is no general legal requirement for non-helm passengers on German federal waterways to remain sober.

What matters is who is at the helm. The **Schiffsführer** (vessel operator) carries full legal responsibility. If it is unclear who is responsible, or if an intoxicated person spontaneously takes the wheel, everyone involved can face legal exposure.

**Plan before you cast off:** Decide before departure who will be at the helm and for which legs of the trip. That person does not drink. End of story.

## Checks on the Water: What to Expect

The Wasserschutzpolizei performs regular checks on German waterways, particularly during summer. A typical check may include:

- Sportbootführerschein and other documents
- Safety equipment (life jackets, fire extinguisher, distress signals)
- Breathalyser test if there is reason to suspect alcohol

A breathalyser test cannot simply be refused when there is reasonable suspicion. If the breath test gives a notable reading, or following any accident, authorities can order a **blood draw**. The blood BAC value is binding – not how you feel.

**Practical tip:** If you are going to take the helm later in the day, do not drink earlier. The body breaks down alcohol at approximately 0.1 to 0.15‰ per hour. Drinking three beers in the afternoon and expecting to be under 1.0‰ by evening is unreliable arithmetic.

## Alcohol, Fatigue, and Sun: A Triple Effect on the Water

On the water, alcohol hits harder than on land, and this is not folklore. Sun exposure, the motion of the boat, water glare, and physical fatigue all amplify the effects of alcohol. Someone who feels fine after two beers on land may be noticeably impaired after the same amount on a busy day afloat.

The subjective feeling of having "sobered up" is not a reliable guide. BAC follows chemistry, not self-perception. Anyone who will be responsible for the helm – even hours later – should avoid alcohol entirely during the trip.

## The SBF Exam Covers This

Alcohol limits and their legal basis are part of the official ELWIS question catalogue and appear regularly in the SBF theory exam. Typical exam questions involve:

- Above which BAC level is a boat operator considered unfit to operate?
- What is the difference between relative and absolute incapacity?
- Which legal provisions apply to alcohol offences on the water?

Confusing the road traffic rule (0.5‰ under the StVO) with the boat rule (1.0‰ based on case law) is a common exam mistake. If you are preparing for the [SBF theory test](/blog/sbf-exam-everything-you-need-to-know/), make sure you know both thresholds and what they mean in practice.

## What About Other Countries?

Germany's alcohol rules apply on German federal waterways. If you plan to cruise abroad – for example, in Croatia, the Netherlands, or Italy – the rules of the host country apply. Some countries have stricter limits (closer to road traffic rules), and enforcement can be rigorous. Always check the specific regulations of your destination before departure.

## Conclusion

Alcohol on the water is not a taboo topic – but if you are at the helm, you must be sober. The thresholds established by German case law (1.0‰ for presumed relative incapacity, 1.6‰ for absolute incapacity) apply on inland waterways and coastal waters alike. Below 1.0‰, visible signs of impairment can still trigger criminal proceedings. The consequences range from fines and criminal charges to the loss of insurance coverage.

The rule is simple: whoever takes the helm does not drink. Whoever wants to drink hands over the helm to a sober person with a valid SBF – and makes that arrangement before casting off.
