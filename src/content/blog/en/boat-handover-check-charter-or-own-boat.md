You passed your Sportbootführerschein (the German recreational boating license, SBF), the boat is waiting at the dock, and everyone is excited. This is exactly the moment when mistakes happen: you want to cast off and simply take the boat as it is. A structured **handover check** usually does not take long and saves you trouble, disputes over damage and, in the worst case, dangerous situations on the water. This article shows what to look at, whether you charter or take over your own boat.

## Why the handover check matters

Your [SBF](/en/sbf-inland/) gives you the authorization to skipper a boat. It does not tell you whether this particular boat is in good condition or whether you can handle it safely. As skipper you are responsible for the boat, the crew and the equipment, and that responsibility starts before you cast off.

When chartering there is a second reason. By signing the **handover protocol** you usually confirm the condition of the boat. Damage you did not document is hard to prove as pre-existing when you return the boat. With your own boat, nobody does the inspection for you. Here self-discipline counts, especially after a purchase or a long time in storage.

## Step 1: Sort out papers and formalities

Before you touch the boat, deal with the organizational side.

- **Contract and handover protocol:** Actually read the documents. What is included in the price, how high is the deposit, what applies in case of damage, and how are deductible and insurance cover regulated?
- **Your license:** Your SBF should be with you on the trip. Depending on the waterway and equipment, a radio certificate may also be required, for example if you use a marine radio on board. More on that in the article about [UBI vs. SRC](/en/blog/ubi-vs-src-radio-license/).
- **Boat documents:** Ask which documents must be on board and where they are kept. What exactly is required depends on the boat and the waterway. Ask the charter company instead of guessing.
- **Insurance:** Clarify what liability and hull cover include and where the limits are. For your own boat, the article on [boat insurance for sportboats](/en/blog/boat-insurance-for-sportboats-what-makes-sense/) is worth a look.
- **Cruising area:** Some charter companies restrict the cruising area or have rules on locks, tidal waters or night trips. Know these limits before you plan your route.

## Step 2: Walk around the boat and document pre-existing damage

Now to the boat itself. Walk all the way around it, ideally together with someone from the charter company, and look at it piece by piece.

- **Hull and superstructure:** Scratches, dents, cracks, blisters, loose fittings. Take photos of anything unusual, including seemingly minor damage.
- **Fenders, lines and lifelines:** Are there enough fenders and mooring lines, and are they in usable condition? Are the stanchions firm?
- **Underwater area:** If the boat is on land or you can see below the waterline, check the propeller and rudder for damage, for example from grounding.
- **Cover and canopy:** Are they complete and intact, and how are they fastened and released?

The key point: **everything you find belongs in the handover protocol.** Have the entries countersigned and keep a copy. Dated photos help as well. That way you avoid being held responsible on return for damage that was already there.

## Step 3: Check the safety equipment

Safety equipment is the part you are most likely to skip out of excitement, and it matters most in an emergency. Check that it is not only present but also within reach and working.

- **Life jackets:** There should be a suitable jacket for every person on board, including children. Check fit, condition and the release mechanism of automatic jackets. Which buoyancy class suits what is explained in the article on [life jacket buoyancy classes](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/).
- **Fire extinguisher:** Present, easy to reach and with a valid inspection tag or indicator? Do you know how to operate it?
- **First aid kit:** Complete and not expired. The article on [first aid on board](/en/blog/first-aid-on-board/) adds useful background.
- **Anchor and anchor line:** Is the anchor within reach, and is the line long enough and firmly attached to the boat?
- **Bailing equipment:** Hand pump, bucket or electric bilge pump. Briefly test the pump if there is one.
- **Signaling devices:** Check which signaling devices are on board and whether they are still within their expiry date. The rules on pyrotechnics are covered in the article on the [pyrotechnics license and distress signals](/en/blog/fkn-pyrotechnic-distress-signals/).
- **Sound signal and lights:** Does the horn or whistle work, and do the navigation lights come on? This matters especially if you might still be out at dusk.

Which equipment is actually required depends on boat type and waterway. Do not rely on "everything is surely there"; compare the equipment against what you need.

## Step 4: Check the engine and consumables

Even without mechanic knowledge you can spot a lot yourself. The goal is not to take the engine apart but to notice obvious problems early.

- **Fuel:** How full is the tank, and what was agreed? Charter boats are often returned with a full tank. Clarify this beforehand.
- **Engine compartment or outboard:** Oil level, visible leaks, loose hoses, condition of the cables. If you are unsure, ask the charter company to show you the checks.
- **Battery and electrics:** Does the engine start reliably, and do instruments, lights and bilge pump work?
- **Bilge:** Is there water in the boat? A little moisture can be normal, but larger amounts are a warning sign.
- **Cooling water:** Once the engine is running, a visible stream of cooling water should exit, provided that is how the engine type is designed. If it does not, shut the engine off immediately.
- **Gas system, heater, stove:** Have the operation explained and switch everything off safely again. Take particular care with gas.
- **Toilet and water tanks:** Understand the operation and the rules on disposal. The article on [environmental protection while boating](/en/blog/environmental-protection-boating-water-conservation/) explains what matters.

The same applies to a used boat of your own, except that you do not have the charter company's briefing. If you have just bought the boat or are starting after the winter break, the [spring checklist](/en/blog/getting-your-boat-ready-spring-checklist-season-start/) is a useful addition.

## Step 5: Briefing and navigation

Take the time for a real briefing, even if the charter company is in a hurry.

- **Have the operation explained:** Starting and stopping the engine, throttle and gearbox, trim, anchor windlass, instruments, radio, chartplotter.
- **Boat quirks:** Every boat has its own character, such as a sensitive helm, a stiff gearshift or a special way of securing lines.
- **Local information:** Where are shallows, narrow passages, locks and fuel docks? Ask about local particularities and look at the chart or pilot book. How to read a chart is covered in the article [how to read a nautical chart](/en/blog/how-to-read-a-nautical-chart/).
- **Weather and trip planning:** Find out the weather situation before you cast off and plan alternative harbors.
- **Emergency contacts:** Write down the charter company's phone number and contact person and brief your crew.

## Step 6: Test run and crew briefing

Before you enter a tight harbor or open water, a short test run in a safe place is worthwhile. Check calmly:

- How do the helm, throttle and reverse respond?
- How does the boat behave at low speed, when turning in a small space and when stopping?
- Do the instruments and the radio work?
- How does the wind affect the boat when you let it drift?

If you need some practice, the article on [docking and undocking for beginners](/en/blog/docking-undocking-harbor-maneuvers/) helps. An honest test in calm water beats your first docking maneuver in a crosswind with an audience.

Just as important is the **crew briefing** before you set off:

- Who wears a life jacket, and when is it put on?
- Where are the fire extinguisher, first aid kit and signaling devices?
- Who can stop the boat or shut off the engine in an emergency?
- What to do in a man overboard situation? The article on the [man overboard maneuver](/en/blog/man-overboard-maneuver/) explains the basics.

The clearer the roles, the calmer everyone stays when something unexpected does happen.

## Don't forget the return

The handover check does not end when you cast off. Plan enough time for the return so you do not arrive in a rush.

- **Refuel** as agreed.
- **Tidy up** and stow the equipment you used.
- **Report damage** that happened during the trip instead of hiding it. Openness is almost always better than discussions afterward.
- **Go through the return protocol** together and have it countersigned.

## Conclusion

A handover check costs a little time and patience, but it protects you from disputes, costs and dangerous surprises. Clarify papers and responsibilities first, document pre-existing damage carefully, check safety equipment and engine, have the boat explained to you and do a test run before the trip really starts. With your own boat the same principle applies, except that you are the inspector yourself.
