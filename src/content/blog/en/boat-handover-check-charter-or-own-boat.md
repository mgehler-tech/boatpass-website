You passed the exam, the boat is at the pontoon, and all you want to do is head out. That is exactly the moment to take a calm look at the boat. A structured **handover check** takes a little time, protects you from unpleasant surprises underway, and with a charter boat saves you from disputes over damage you did not cause. This article shows what to check before you cast off for the first time.

## Why the check is more than a formality

When you take over, you become the **person responsible on board**. That covers navigation and driving as much as the condition of the boat and its equipment. If the engine fails underway, the bilge pump does not start, or there are no suitable life jackets on board, "it was already like that" does not help much on the water.

With a **charter boat** there is a second layer: existing scratches, dents or broken parts belong in the handover protocol before you leave. Anything not written there may be disputed when you return the boat. With **your own boat** the same principle applies, except there is no charter company across the table. You alone are responsible for making sure papers, technology and safety equipment are in order. If you are still deciding which route suits you, the article [Own Boat or Charter Boat After the SBF](/en/blog/own-boat-or-charter-boat-sbf/) can help.

## Step 1: Clarify papers and responsibilities

Before looking at the boat itself, sort out the formalities. It pays not to take anything for granted:

- **Your own license:** Does your license match the boat and the cruising area? Which license applies where is explained in [SBF Validity: Which License Where](/en/blog/sbf-validity-which-license-where/).
- **Boat papers:** Ask which documents belong to the boat and should be on board. Exactly which are required depends on the area, boat type and ownership. If in doubt, ask the charter company or the responsible authority instead of guessing.
- **Insurance:** With charter, ask what the company's insurance covers and what deductible applies in case of damage. For your own boat, [Boat Insurance for Sport Boats](/en/blog/boat-insurance-for-sportboats-what-makes-sense/) explains what matters.
- **Cruising area and restrictions:** Are there areas the company excludes, or rules on weather, night trips and crew?
- **Contacts:** Note the number for breakdowns and emergencies at the charter company or harbor office.

## Step 2: Walk around the boat

Walk around the boat once, ideally with the charter staff or the previous owner. The overall impression matters as much as the details.

### Hull and superstructure

Look for **visible damage** on the hull: cracks, scratches, dents or traces of earlier repairs. Check the rub rails, windows, hatches and fittings. Does everything open and close properly? On a used boat you intend to buy, signs of moisture inside are something to take seriously.

Take **photos from all sides**, including existing damage. With date and time, they are your best documentation if questions come up later.

### Lines, fenders and anchor

Are there **enough lines and fenders**, and are they in usable condition? Check the cleats and fittings where lines are made fast. The ground tackle includes the anchor, chain or rope, and a way to handle it safely. How anchoring works is covered in [Anchoring Basics: Anchor Types and Common Mistakes](/en/blog/anchoring-basics-anchor-types-mistakes/). If there is an anchor windlass, have it demonstrated briefly.

## Step 3: Safety equipment

This deserves particular care, because in the worst case you will really need it. Go through the list and check not only whether the items are there, but whether you can operate them.

- **Life-saving equipment:** There should be a suitable life jacket available for every person on board, in the right size, for example for children. Check for visible defects and have the jackets explained. More in [Choosing the Right Life Jacket](/en/blog/life-jacket-buoyancy-classes/).
- **Fire extinguishers:** Where are they, and do they look ready for use? Is there any sign of an inspection?
- **First aid kit:** Is it complete and accessible? Basics are in [First Aid on Board](/en/blog/first-aid-on-board/).
- **Signaling devices and tools:** Which signals are on board, and are they still in date? For pyrotechnics see [Pyrotechnic Distress Signals](/en/blog/fkn-pyrotechnic-distress-signals/). Basic tools and a spare parts kit are useful too.
- **Bailing:** Do the bilge pump or hand pump work, and do you know where they are?
- **Communication:** Is there a VHF radio or another means of communication on board, and how is it operated? Basics are in [Marine Radio Basics for Beginners](/en/blog/marine-radio-basics-beginners/).

If something is missing or damaged, raise it immediately and have it noted in the protocol or replaced. Legal minimum equipment requirements depend on the rules for your cruising area, so if in doubt ask the charter company or the responsible authority.

## Step 4: Engine and technology

Even without mechanical training, you can do a meaningful **basic check**. What matters is going through it systematically.

### Engine and fuel

- **Fuel level:** How full is the tank, and what is the rule on return? Note the level in the protocol.
- **Oil and cooling:** Ask whether oil level and coolant have been checked, and have it shown how to check them yourself. For outboards, see [Outboard Motor Basic Maintenance](/en/blog/outboard-motor-basic-maintenance/).
- **Test run:** Let the engine run while stationary. Listen for odd noises, watch for smoke, and check that cooling water comes out of the outlet where the model provides one.
- **Throttle and shift:** Does everything move smoothly? Where is neutral, and how do you engage reverse?
- **Kill switch:** If the boat has a lanyard or emergency stop, have it shown how it works.

### Electrics, lights and instruments

Test the **navigation lights**, the bilge pump and every consumer you will need underway. This applies to planned day trips too, since unexpected delays can push you into dusk. What each light means is explained in [Navigation Lights Explained](/en/blog/navigation-lights-explained/). Also check the depth sounder, compass, chartplotter and radio where fitted. How modern navigation works together is covered in [GPS, Chartplotter and VHF on Board](/en/blog/gps-chartplotter-vhf-modern-navigation/).

### Steering

Turn the helm from lock to lock and watch for smoothness and play. Ask whether the boat has any quirks, such as pronounced prop walk or sluggish response in reverse. An unfamiliar type of boat handles differently from the one you practiced on.

## Step 5: Briefing and test run

A good charter company offers a **briefing**. Use it actively: do not only listen, operate the important things yourself, from starting the engine to using the pumps and the radio. Ask about local features, such as shallows, currents or narrow passages near the harbor.

If possible, do a **short test run** in a sheltered area. Try starting off, stopping, reversing and a turn in tight space. That helps you judge the boat's behavior before it counts. For first maneuvers in harbor, [Docking and Undocking: Harbor Maneuvers for Beginners](/en/blog/docking-undocking-harbor-maneuvers/) is a good addition. Talking through a man-overboard maneuver with your crew also pays off, see [Man Overboard Maneuver](/en/blog/man-overboard-maneuver/).

## Step 6: Handover protocol and return

The **handover protocol** is your most important document. It should contain at least:

- the condition of hull and superstructure, referring to known damage,
- the fuel level and, where relevant, the water level,
- the equipment on board,
- known defects and open points,
- date, time and the signatures of both sides.

Get a copy and keep your **photos**. On return, the same process runs in reverse: inspect the boat together, note defects, clarify fuel. If damage or incidents occur underway, report and document them immediately. What to do after an accident is covered in [Conduct After a Boating Accident](/en/blog/conduct-after-a-boating-accident-duties-and-reporting/).

## Special points for a boat you bought

With your own boat you carry more responsibility but also have more freedom. Besides the steps above, a few points matter especially:

- **Maintenance history:** Ask to see past invoices, service reports and notes on repairs.
- **Independent survey:** For larger purchases, a survey by an independent professional can make sense, particularly if you feel technically unsure.
- **Ownership and papers:** Make sure all documents are complete and in the right name. If unsure, get professional advice.
- **Winter storage and season start:** How to prepare the boat for the season is in [Getting Your Boat Ready in Spring](/en/blog/getting-your-boat-ready-spring-checklist-season-start/).

## Practical checklist

So you forget nothing, here is the flow in short:

1. Clarify license, boat papers, insurance and contact numbers.
2. Walk around: check and photograph hull, superstructure, lines, fenders, anchor.
3. Check safety equipment: jackets, extinguishers, first aid, signals, bilge pump, radio.
4. Test technology: fuel, oil, test run, lights, instruments, steering.
5. Use the briefing and, if possible, do a short test run.
6. Have the handover protocol signed with all defects listed.
7. Plan weather and route for the first trip calmly.

## Conclusion

A handover check costs a little time but saves stress, money and, in an emergency, safety. If you check papers, equipment and technology systematically, use the briefing actively and take the handover protocol seriously, you start your first trip more relaxed. That holds for a charter boat as much as for your own. The check does not replace a professional survey or a question to the charter company, but it is a solid foundation.
