You may already know that channel 16 is the calling and distress channel and what MAYDAY means. The tricky part starts when you sit at the microphone for the first time: What do you say, and in which order? When do you switch channels? How do you end the conversation? This article walks you through a typical radio exchange with **example dialogues** and explains the rules behind it, so that radio procedure is second nature in real life and in the exam.

A quick note up front: the examples follow **VHF marine radio** and its internationally common procedure. The exact wording, the channel plan for your area and the current rules are set out in the official question catalogue and in local waterway information. The dialogues here are **learning examples**, not a script to memorize word for word. (In inland radio, German is usually spoken, with "hier ist" instead of "this is". In Germany, the radio certificates are the SRC for sea and the UBI for inland waters, both separate from the Sportbootführerschein, the recreational boating license.)

## The basic idea: short, clear, one sender at a time

Radio is an exchange. Only one station can transmit on a channel at a time while everyone else listens. This leads to the basic rules of **radio discipline**:

- **Think first, then press.** Know what you want to say before you transmit.
- **Press the key, pause briefly, then speak.** Otherwise the start of your message is cut off.
- **Speak slowly, clearly and at normal volume.** Shouting distorts more than it helps.
- **Release the key** when you are done. Only then can the other station reply.
- **Short sentences and fixed terms** instead of long stories.

Fixed terms include so-called **procedure words**. The most important ones are "over", "out", "say again" and "received" or "understood". The difference between "over" and "out" matters: **Over** means "I am done, it is your turn". **Out** means "The conversation is finished, I expect no reply". Saying both together ("over and out") is therefore contradictory and not correct procedure.

## Step 1: The call

A call has three building blocks: **who** you are calling, **who** you are, and a sign to answer. Here is an example of a ship-to-ship call:

> **Blue Gull:** "Seastar, Seastar, Seastar, this is Blue Gull, Blue Gull, Blue Gull, over."

"This is" separates the called station from the calling station. For safe identification, the name is repeated at **first contact**, at most three times. Once contact is established, saying the name once or using a short form is enough. When in doubt, it is better to be once too clear than once too unclear.

The **channel** matters: you make your first call on the designated **calling channel** (on VHF marine radio this is channel 16) or, if your set supports it, by **DSC individual call**. No long conversation takes place on channel 16, which leads to the next step.

## Step 2: The reply and the switch to a working channel

If the called station has heard you, it replies. The order is reversed: first it says **your** name, then its own. It can also propose a working channel straight away:

> **Seastar:** "Blue Gull, this is Seastar, go to channel 72, over."

You acknowledge, switch to the proposed channel and call again there:

> **Blue Gull:** "Seastar, this is Blue Gull, channel 72, over."

Channel "72" is only an **example** of a working channel between vessels. Which channels are meant for which purpose is set out in the official documents. What matters is the principle: the calling channel stays occupied only for making contact and is freed again quickly. If the calling station proposes the working channel, the roles are simply swapped.

## Step 3: The actual conversation

On the working channel you can now exchange your information, for example an agreement about overtaking or a question about a berth. Again: short and unambiguous. An example:

> **Blue Gull:** "Seastar, this is Blue Gull. I will pass you on your port side, over."
> **Seastar:** "Blue Gull, this is Seastar. Understood, port side, over."

If you have **not understood** something, say so openly: "Say again, please, over." Repeat important details such as positions, numbers and channels so that misunderstandings show up. For unclear names or words, the **international spelling alphabet** (Alfa, Bravo, Charlie and so on) helps. How digits are pronounced is part of the radio training for your certificate.

## Step 4: Ending the call

When everything is settled, you end the conversation cleanly, so that both sides know it is over and the channel is free again.

> **Blue Gull:** "Seastar, this is Blue Gull. Thank you, out."
> **Seastar:** "Blue Gull, this is Seastar, out."

The word **out** ends the conversation. After that, you switch your radio back to **channel 16** so that you can keep listening for calls and distress messages. Forgetting this switch is a classic beginner gap.

## The distress call: the sequence you must know

The most important radio traffic of all is the distress call. The procedure is strictly structured so that nothing is missed even under stress. On a DSC-capable set, you first trigger the **DSC distress alert** (sent on channel 70) and then make the **voice distress call on channel 16**. It follows this structure:

1. **MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY** (three times)
2. **THIS IS** and the **vessel name** (three times), plus call sign or MMSI
3. **MAYDAY**, vessel name, call sign or MMSI (once)
4. The **position**: latitude and longitude, or bearing and distance to a known point
5. The **nature of distress**, for example fire, taking on water or man overboard
6. The **assistance required**
7. The **number of persons** on board
8. Any other useful information such as vessel type, color or life-saving equipment
9. **OVER**

A shortened example, as it might sound in a drill:

> "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. This is Blue Gull, Blue Gull, Blue Gull, MMSI (nine digits). MAYDAY Blue Gull, MMSI. Position: 2 nautical miles north of the lighthouse. Taking on water, sinking. Require immediate assistance. Four persons on board. Over."

A distress call is not about elegance but about **completeness and calm**. The key points are where you are, what has happened and how many people are affected. Afterwards you **stay at the radio** and wait for the answer. If your call is not acknowledged, you repeat it. ## Typical mistakes in radio practice

Many mistakes keep coming back among beginners:

- **Talking too long on channel 16.** It is not a conversation channel, so switch to a working channel quickly.
- **Releasing the key too early or pressing it too late.** The start or end of the message is lost.
- **Not switching back to channel 16 after the call.** You then miss distress calls.
- **Wrong urgency level.** MAYDAY is only for immediate danger to vessel or people. A false distress call is no minor offense.
- **Unclear names.** In poor reception, spell them instead of hoping.
- **Too much information at once.** Better to send in small, clear steps and let the other station acknowledge.

## How to practice radio without a real set

You learn radio procedure best through **repeating it out loud**. Play the dialogues with a study partner, ideally back to back so that you can only hear each other. Write a small distress card with the order of the building blocks and learn it until you can recite it without notes. Also practice **spelling** your boat's name. The moment you have to read something out under time pressure throws many candidates, even though they know the material.

## Conclusion

A radio conversation always follows the same pattern: **call** on the calling channel, **reply** with a channel proposal, **switch** to the working channel, a short **conversation** with clear terms and finally a clean **close** with "out" and a switch back to channel 16. The **distress call** follows an even stricter scheme that you should master before you need it. If you have practiced the sequences out loud, you will radio calmly day to day and purposefully in an emergency.
